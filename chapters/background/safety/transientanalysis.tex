There are several different methods which have been used for analyzing transients in \glspl{MSR}.
These methods include, as of 2018, "...fluid-flow-velocity field
coupled multi-group, time-resolved, neutron diffusion; ... point reactor kinetics schemes; and
quasi-statics; generally all coupled, tightly or loosely, to a thermal-hydraulics solver" \cite{wooten2018review}.

\subsubsection{Multi-group diffusion}

Multi-group diffusion kinetics methods are among the most accurate, and computationally expensive, approaches which can be applied to transient problems.
Hurwitz and Ehrlich have a general formulation of multi-group diffusion written in 1953, and many more recent works have used the method \cite{hurwitz1953multigroup}.

Examples of works that use fluid-flow-velocity field coupled multi-group include GenFOAM and Griffin/Pronghorn coupling using \gls{MOOSE} \cite{fiorina2015gen, tano2023coupled}.
Other examples can be found in the review by Wooten at al. \cite{kvrepel2007dyn3d, fiorina2014modelling, kophazi2009development}.
Even more works which use the multi-group diffusion method can be found in the literature \cite{zhang2009analysis, yamamoto2006transient, krepel2007dyn3d, nicolino2008coupled}

These works typically use Picard iteration with multi-group neutron diffusion coupled with thermal-hydraulics to accurately capture the multi-physics effects of the reactor.
However, this accuracy also comes with a higher computational cost than simpler methods.
The general form of the coupling can be seen in Equations \eqref{eq:mg-n-diff} and \eqref{eq:mg-n-diff-Ci} \cite{wooten2018review}.


\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \frac{1}{\nu_g} \frac{\partial \phi_g}{\partial t} = & \; \nabla \cdot D_g \nabla \phi_g + \sum_{g' \neq g}^G \Sigma_{s, g' \rightarrow g} \phi_{g'} \\
    & + (1-\beta) \chi_{p, g} \sum_{g'=1}^{G} (\nu \Sigma_f)_{g'} \phi_{g'}\\
    & \sum_{i=1}^I \chi_{d, g, i} \lambda_i C_i - \Sigma_{a, g} \phi_g - \sum_{g' \neq g}^G \Sigma_{s, g \rightarrow g'} \phi_{g}
    \label{eq:mg-n-diff}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial C_i}{\partial t} = \beta_i \sum_{g=1}^G (\nu \Sigma_f)_g \phi_g - \lambda_i C_i - \nabla \cdot (C_i \vec{u}) + \nabla \cdot \frac{\nu_T}{Sc_T} \nabla C_i
    \label{eq:mg-n-diff-Ci}
\end{equation}

The terms in Equations \eqref{eq:mg-n-diff} and \eqref{eq:mg-n-diff-Ci} are defined as follows:
\begin{align}
\nu_g &= \mbox{ the average number of neutrons produced by fission of group \textit{g} [-]} \nonumber\\
\phi_g &= \mbox{ the scalar neutron flux of group \textit{g} [$\frac{n}{cm^2 s}$]} \nonumber\\
D_g &=\mbox{ the group \textit{g} diffusion term [$cm$]}\nonumber\\
G &= \mbox{ the total number of energy groups [-]} \nonumber\\
\Sigma_{s, g' \rightarrow g} &= \mbox{the macroscopic scattering cross section from group \textit{g'} to group \textit{g} [$cm^{-1}$]} \nonumber\\
\beta &= \mbox{the delayed neutron fraction [-]} \nonumber\\
\chi_{p, g} &= \mbox{the number of prompt neutrons born in group \textit{g} [-]} \nonumber\\
\Sigma_f &= \mbox{the macroscopic fission cross section [$cm^{-1}$]} \nonumber\\
I &= \mbox{the number of delayed neutron precursor groups [-]} \nonumber\\
\chi_{d, g, i} &= \mbox{the number of delayed neutrons from precursor group \textit{i} born in group \textit{g} [-]} \nonumber\\
\lambda_i &= \mbox{the decay constant for group \textit{i} [$s^{-1}$]} \nonumber\\
C_i &= \mbox{the concentration of group \textit{i} [$\frac{atoms}{cm^3}$]} \nonumber\\
\vec{u} &= \mbox{the velocity profile [$\frac{cm}{s}$]} \nonumber\\
\Sigma_{a, g} &= \mbox{the macroscopic absorption cross section for group \textit{g} [$cm^{-1}]$} \nonumber\\
\beta_i &= \mbox{the delayed neutron fraction for group \textit{it} [-]} \nonumber\\
\nu_T &= \mbox{the eddy viscosity [$\frac{cm^2}{s}]$} \nonumber\\
Sc_T &= \mbox{the turbulent Schmidt number [-].} \nonumber
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Modified point kinetics}

\Gls{PRK} are a simpler approach to modeling a transient, with the basic form given in Equations \eqref{eq:prk-n} and \eqref{eq:prk-C}.
The derivation of the \gls{PRK} equations can be found in the work by Henry, with a fluid-fueled derivation in the work by Lapenta et al. \cite{henry1958application, lapenta2001point}.
\Gls{MPK} are used for \glspl{MSR}, as standard \gls{PRK} do not properly handle the movement of the fuel salt.

\begin{equation}
    \frac{dn(t)}{dt} = \frac{\rho(t) - \beta_{eff}}{\Lambda} n(t) + \sum_{i=1}^I \lambda_i C_i(t)
    \label{eq:prk-n}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{dC_i(t)}{dt} = \frac{\beta_{eff, i}}{\Lambda} n(t) - \lambda_i C_i(t)
    \label{eq:prk-C}
\end{equation}

The terms in Equations \eqref{eq:prk-n} and \eqref{eq:prk-C} are defined as follows:
\begin{align}
n &= \mbox{ the relative neutron density [-]} \nonumber\\
\rho &= \mbox{ the reactivity [-]} \nonumber\\
\beta_{eff} &= \mbox{ the effective delayed neutron fraction [-]} \nonumber\\
\Lambda &= \mbox{ the neutron generation time [$s$]} \nonumber\\
\lambda_i &= \mbox{ the group \textit{i} decay constant [$s^{-1}$]} \nonumber\\
C_i &= \mbox{ the concentration of group \textit{i} [$atoms$]} \nonumber\\
\beta_{eff, i} &= \mbox{ the effective delayed neutron fraction from group \textit{i} [-].} \nonumber
\end{align}

%\begin{description}
%    \item $n$ is the relative neutron density
%    \item $\rho$ is the reactivity
%    \item $\beta_{eff}$ is the effective delayed neutron fraction
%    \item $\Lambda$ is the prompt neutron generation time
%    \item $\lambda_i$ is the group $i$ decay constant
%    \item $C_i$ is the concentration of group $i$
%    \item $\beta_{eff, i}$ is the effective delayed neutron fraction from group $i$
%\end{description}

\Gls{PRK} in general struggle to capture spatial effects, such as flow variation, which is important for \glspl{MSR}. 
\Gls{PRK} do, however, give a correct prediction for general evolution trends \cite{wooten2018review}.
Wooten et al. provide four different works which use \gls{MPK} in \gls{MSR} analysis \cite{guo2013simulations, merle2015physical, shi2016development, zanetti2015extension}.

The work by Guo et al. analyzes \gls{ULOHS} and \gls{ULOF} transients in \gls{MOSART}.
The \gls{PRK} equations are modified into the \gls{DPK}, which model precursor concentrations in-core and ex-core.
These equations, shown in Equations \eqref{eq:dpk-n} through \eqref{eq:dpk-cex} (recreated from \cite{guo2013simulations, shi2016development}), are also used by Shi et al. in \gls{RELAP} for a load demand change, primary flow transient, secondary flow transient, and reactivity transient in the \gls{MSBR} \cite{shi2016development}.
This approach enables modeling of ex-core decay of \glspl{DNP}, capturing effects at power and for loss of flow, but "...may fail to adequately capture reactivity oscillations in transients
occurring at low or zero power and with increases in flow rate" \cite{wooten2018review}.


\begin{equation}
    \frac{dn(t)}{dt} = \frac{\rho - \tilde{\beta}_{eff}}{\Lambda} n(t) + \sum_{i=1}^{I} \lambda_i C_{i, core} (t)
    \label{eq:dpk-n}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \frac{dC_{i, core}(t)}{dt} & = \frac{\beta_i}{\Lambda} n(t) - \left(\lambda_i + \frac{1}{\tau_{core}} \right) C_{i, core}(t)\\
    & + \frac{1}{\tau_{loop}} \left(\frac{V_{loop}}{V_{core}}\right) C_{i, loop} (t - \tau_{loop})\\
    \label{eq:dpk-cin}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{dC_{i, loop}(t)}{dt} = \frac{1}{\tau_{core}} \left(\frac{V_{core}}{V_{loop}}\right) C_{i, core} (t) - \left(\lambda_i + \frac{1}{\tau_{loop}} \right) C_{i, loop}(t)
    \label{eq:dpk-cex}
\end{equation}

Intermediate terms used in Equations \eqref{eq:dpk-n} through \eqref{eq:dpk-cex} are shown in Equations \eqref{eq:dpk-betaeff} through \eqref{eq:dpk-tauloop}.

\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\beta}_{eff} = \beta_{static} - \beta_{loss}
    \label{eq:dpk-betaeff}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \beta_{loss} = \frac{\Lambda(t)}{n(t)} \sum_{i=1}^I \lambda_i C_{i, loop}
    \label{eq:dpk-betaloss}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \tau_{core} = \frac{M_{core}}{w(t)}
    \label{eq:dpk-taucore}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \tau_{core} = \frac{M_{loop}}{w(t)}
    \label{eq:dpk-tauloop}
\end{equation}

The terms in Equations \eqref{eq:dpk-n} through \eqref{eq:dpk-tauloop} are defined as follows, with most terms identical to those in the \gls{PRK} equations:
\begin{align}
V_{loop} &= \mbox{ the volume of the loop [$cm^3$]} \nonumber \\ 
V_{core} &= \mbox{ the volume of the core [$cm^3$]} \nonumber \\ 
\beta_{static} &= \mbox{ the value of $\beta_{eff}$ without flow [-]} \nonumber \\ 
M_{core} &= \mbox{ the mass in the core [$kg$]} \nonumber \\ 
M_{loop} &= \mbox{ the mass in the loop [$kg$]} \nonumber \\ 
w(t) &= \mbox{ the mass flow rate [$\frac{kg}{s}$].} \nonumber
\end{align}

A similar method is also used by Suzuki and Shimazu for analyzing a reactivity initiated accident in the FUJI-12 reactor \cite{suzuki2008reactivity}.
That work uses a simpler form of the equations, without dividing the precursor concentrations into core and loop regions.

The work by Merle-Lucotte et al. uses a method called "IPK method," which may stand for "Improve Point-Kinetic method," though this is not explicitly stated \cite{merle2015physical}.
This method uses a fixed mesh for neutronics and a mobile mesh for fluid properties.
Merle-Lucotte et al. found that the \gls{PRK} method without any modifications was able to match the IPK method for general trends and end-states, but was unable to capture reactivity oscillations properly.
The IPK method was able to capture these oscillations, but assumes "a uniform fission distribution in the core, together with an instantaneous propagation and extraction of the heat produced" \cite{merle2015physical}.

The work by Zanetti et al. creates an extension of the TRACE code, a finite volume code used for \gls{LWR} transients \cite{zanetti2015extension}.
The \gls{DNP} concentration as a function of space and time is resolved upwind in space and with backward Euler for time. 
Adjoint-weighted quantities discretized with the trapezoidal rule are then passed to \gls{PRK} equation, and the set of \gls{DNP} concentration and the \gls{PRK} power, or neutron density, equations are resolved using LU factorization.

Another approach to modify the \gls{PRK} is to alter the kinetics parameters based on a phase-space integration weighted with the adjoint flux \cite{lapenta2000basic}.
Alternatively, effective flow-inclusive group yields can be calculated, as shown by Haubenreich, and used in \gls{PRK} equations \cite{haubenreich1962prediction}.

\subsubsection{Quasi-statics}

Quasi-static methods seek to maintain the accuracy of multi-group diffusion approaches while also having the cost savings of \gls{PRK}.
The general approach of these methods is to update \gls{PRK} parameters with occasional multi-group diffusion simulations to capture changes in flux and \gls{DNP} spatial distributions \cite{wooten2018review}.
A complete derivation of the quasi-static method can be found in the work by Henry, who discusses the shape and amplitude flux functions, and the work by Devooght, who discusses the  generalized quasi-static method \cite{henry1958application, devooght1980quasistatic}.
There are also other works which discuss the quasi-static method in detail \cite{becker1968generalized}.

Wooten et al. introduce several different works which have used quasi-statics \cite{wooten2018review}.
One of these, from 2005 by Rineiski et al., covers the SIMMER code, which couples neutronics, thermal-hydraulics, and structure codes \cite{rineiski2005kinetics}.
The neutron flux is treated as shown in Equation \eqref{eq:flux-treatment-rineiski}, where there is a flux amplitude, $N(t)$, which is recalculated more frequently than the flux shape, $\Phi (\vec{x}, t)$.
The neutron flux is constrained in the work by a term containing the phase-space integration and the weighting function used.

\begin{equation}
    \Phi(\vec{x}, t) = N(t) \psi (\vec{x}, t)
    \label{eq:flux-treatment-rineiski}
\end{equation}

Rineiski et al. solve "shape" steps to handle the neutronics and \gls{DNP} spatial concentrations.
The equations used in the "shape" steps are recreated here in Equations \eqref{eq:rineiski-flux} and \eqref{eq:rineiski-big-C}.
These equations are solved by time-discretizing Equation \eqref{eq:rineiski-flux} into a set of steady-state-like equations, which are then treated using the DANTSYS module of SIMMER \cite{alcouffe1995dantsys}.

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \frac{1}{\nu} \frac{\partial \psi (\vec{x}, t)}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{\nu N} \frac{d N}{dt} \psi (\vec{x}, t) + M(t) \psi (\vec{x}, t) = & \\
    (1 - \beta) \chi_p F(t) \psi (\vec{x}, t)
    + \frac{1}{N} \chi_d \sum_{i=1}^I \lambda_i C_i (\vec{x}, t) + \frac{1}{N} Q (\vec{x}, t) & \\
    \label{eq:rineiski-flux}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial C_i (\vec{x}, t)}{\partial t} = N \beta_i F \psi (\vec{x}, t) - \lambda_i C_i (\vec{x}, t) - \nabla ( u (\vec{x}, t) C_i (\vec{x}, t))
    \label{eq:rineiski-big-C}
\end{equation}

Within these "shape" steps are also sub-steps referred to as "reactivity" steps.
At each of these "reactivity" steps, the \gls{PRK} parameters are calculated as well as the flux amplitude.
The equations used in the "reactivity" steps are recreated here in Equations \eqref{eq:rineiski-n} through \eqref{eq:rineiski-cm}.

\begin{equation}
    \frac{dN}{dt} = \left( \frac{\rho - \beta_{eff}}{\Lambda} \right) N + q_{eff} + \sum_{i=1}^I \lambda_i \left( c_{s, i} + c_{m, i} \right)
    \label{eq:rineiski-n}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{dc_{s, i}}{dt} = \frac{\beta_{eff}}{\Lambda} N - \lambda_i C_{s, i}
    \label{eq:rineiski-cs}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{\left\langle W(\vec{x}), \left(\chi F(t) - M(t)\right) \psi  (\vec{x}, t) \right\rangle}{\left\langle W(\vec{x}), \chi F(t) \psi  (\vec{x}, t) \right\rangle}
    \label{eq:rineiski-rho}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    c_{m, i} = \frac{\left\langle W(\vec{x}), \chi_d C_{m, i} \right\rangle}{\Lambda \left\langle W(\vec{x}), \chi F(t) \psi  (\vec{x}, t) \right\rangle}
    \label{eq:rineiski-cm}
\end{equation}

Intermediate terms used in Equations \eqref{eq:rineiski-n} through \eqref{eq:rineiski-cm} are shown in Equations \eqref{eq:rineiski-big-cm} through \eqref{eq:rineiski-big-cs}.
The $C_s(\vec{x}, t)$ term is computed at steady state by setting the left-hand side of both Equation \eqref{eq:rineiski-flux} and \eqref{eq:rineiski-big-C} to zero as well as the fluid velocity to zero at t=0.
This allows Equation \eqref{eq:rineiski-big-cm} to be solved.

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial C_{m, i}(\vec{x}, t)}{\partial t} = - \nabla \left( u(\vec{x}, t) C_{s, i} (\vec{x}, t) \right) - \lambda_i C_{m, i}(\vec{x}, t) - \nabla \left( u(\vec{x}, t) C_{m, i}(\vec{x}, t)\right)
    \label{eq:rineiski-big-cm}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial C_{s, i}(\vec{x}, t)}{\partial t} = N(t) \beta_i F(t) \psi (\vec{x}, t) - \lambda_i \partial C_{s, i}(\vec{x}, t)
    \label{eq:rineiski-big-cs}
\end{equation}

The terms in Equations \eqref{eq:flux-treatment-rineiski} through \eqref{eq:rineiski-big-cs} are defined as follows:
\begin{align}
\Phi &= \mbox{ the neutron flux [$\frac{n}{cm^2 s}$] } \nonumber \\
N(t) &= \mbox{ the neutron flux amplitude [$n$]} \nonumber \\
\psi(\vec{x}, t) &= \mbox{ the neutron flux shape [$cm^{-2} s^{-1}$]} \nonumber \\
\nu &= \mbox{ the average neutron speed [$\frac{cm}{s}$]} \nonumber \\
M(t) &= \mbox{ the neutron disappearance operator [$cm^{-1}$] } \nonumber \\
F(t) &= \mbox{ the neutron production operator [$cm^{-1}$]} \nonumber \\
\beta &= \mbox{ the delayed neutron fraction [-]} \nonumber \\
\beta_{eff} &= \mbox{ the effective delayed neutron fraction [-]} \nonumber \\
\chi_p &= \mbox{ the prompt neutron energy spectra [-]} \nonumber \\
\chi_d &= \mbox{ the delayed neutron energy spectra [-]} \nonumber \\
\chi &= \mbox{ the neutron energy spectra [-]} \nonumber \\
\lambda_i &= \mbox{ the decay constant for \gls{DNP} group \textit{i} [$s^{-1}$] } \nonumber \\
C_i(\vec{x}, t) &= \mbox{ the concentration of \gls{DNP} group \textit{i} [$\frac{atoms}{cm^3}$] } \nonumber \\
Q(\vec{x}, t) &= \mbox{ the external neutron source [$\frac{n}{cm^3 s}$] } \nonumber \\
u(\vec{x}, t) &= \mbox{ the \gls{DNP} velocity [$\frac{cm}{s}$]} \nonumber \\
C_{s, i}(\vec{x}, t) &= \mbox{ the "standard" precursor concentration of group \textit{i} [$\frac{atoms}{cm^3}$] } \nonumber \\
C_{m, i}(\vec{x}, t) &= \mbox{ the "movable" precursor concentration of group \textit{i} [$\frac{atoms}{cm^3}$] } \nonumber \\
\rho &= \mbox{ the reactivity [-]} \nonumber \\
c_{s, i} &= \mbox{ the \gls{PRK} "standard" precursor concentration of group $i$ [$atoms$]} \nonumber \\
c_{m, i} &= \mbox{ the \gls{PRK} "movable" precursor concentration of group $i$ [$atoms$]} \nonumber \\
q_{eff} &= \mbox{ the \gls{PRK} external neutron source [$\frac{n}{s}$] } \nonumber \\
\Lambda &= \mbox{ the neutron generation time [$s$]} \nonumber \\
W(x) &= \mbox{ the weighting function (the default in SIMMER}\\
&\;\;\;\;\; \mbox{ is an adjoint flux computed at steady-state conditions) [-].} \nonumber
\end{align}
