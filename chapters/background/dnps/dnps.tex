An important consideration in nuclear reactors, especially in \glspl{MSR}, are \glspl{DNP}, discovered in 1939, with initial spectral measurements in 1946 \cite{roberts1939further, das1993importance, burgy1946energies}.
\Glspl{DNP} are nuclides which decay via $\beta^-$ decay, producing a daughter, called an emitter, which emits a neutron \cite{bank2000jef}.
In this work, the term \gls{DNP} will be solely used, as the delay of the neutron emission practically is only due to the beta decay \cite{bohr1939mechanism, ott1985introductory}.
Classifying between the emitter and the \gls{DNP} is not relevant for this work.

This delay in the emission of a neutron has a large effect on the reactor period and transient behavior.
The time-dependent rate of delayed neutron emission from a single beta-delayed neutron emitting nuclide, or \gls{DNP}, is shown in Equation \eqref{eq:dnpsingleeq}.

\begin{equation}
    n_{d, i}(t) = P_{n, i} \lambda_i N_i(t)
    \label{eq:dnpsingleeq}
\end{equation}

The terms in Equation \eqref{eq:dnpsingleeq} is defined as follows:
\begin{align}
n_{d, i} &= \mbox{ the delayed neutron activity, or count rate, from \gls{DNP} $i$ [$\frac{n}{s}$]} \nonumber \\
P_{n, i} &= \mbox{ the average number of delayed neutrons emitted from}\\
& \;\;\;\;\;\; \mbox{a single beta decay of nuclide $i$, or the emission probability [-]} \nonumber \\
\lambda_i &= \mbox{ the decay constant of \gls{DNP} $i$ [$s^{-1}$]} \nonumber \\
N_i(t) &= \mbox{ the concentration of \gls{DNP} $i$ [$atoms$].} \nonumber
\end{align}

A challenging aspect of treating \glspl{DNP} in \glspl{MSR} is the necessary inclusion of spatial resolution due to the movement of the fuel salt.
In a solid fuel reactor, such as an \gls{LWR}, a spatial component is not necessary to properly model the \glspl{DNP}.
Modeling space and time together causes computational costs to increase significantly, making problems involving \glspl{DNP} in \glspl{MSR} more challenging than handling \glspl{DNP} in stationary fuels.
Although it is challenging, it is important to properly model the \glspl{DNP} in reactors.

The \glspl{DNP} are important to model properly for several reasons.
Material accountancy, reactivity worth, decay heat, and reactor controllability (particularly for \glspl{MSR}) are all key aspects to understand for safe reactor operation \cite{das1993importance, das1977kinetics, dickens1991analysis, lane1958fluid}.
Without properly modeling \glspl{DNP}, these aspects will not be accurately predicted.
This can lead to either over-conservatism or under-conservatism in safety systems, which increases costs, difficulties in licensing, and risk.

\subsection{Measurement}
\label{sec:measurementofdnps}

There are two approaches used for measurement of \glspl{DNP}, referred to as the macroscopic and microscopic measurement approaches \cite{parish1999status, rudstam2002delayed}.
The approaches differ initially, as the macroscopic approach uses a fissile sample irradiation to get delayed neutron counts which can be approximated using groups.
The microscopic approach instead uses the data from many experiments combined together to simulate a fissile sample irradiation, using the data to generate the delayed neutron counts.
This makes the microscopic approach more challenging, as it depends on the work of many experimentalists, though it also offers more detail on the delayed neutron counts.

\subsubsection{Macroscopic Measurement of Delayed Neutron Precursors}
This section covers the macroscopic approach.
Analysis and collection of macroscopic approach data is very common in the literature, as the data from this method is important for reactors \cite{keepin1957delayed, tuttle1975delayed, manero1972status, benedetti1982delayed, waldo1981delayed, brady1988evaluation, hughes1948delayed, loaiza1998measurements, loaiza2000dominant}.
The macroscopic approach entails irradiation of a sample containing a single fissile nuclide at or near a given neutron energy.
Small samples are used in order to minimize neutron multiplication within the sample.
Two different types of irradiation are used: pulse irradiation, where the irradiation time is shorter than the shortest half-life; and infinite, where the irradiation time is longer than the longest half-life.
After irradiation, the delayed neutrons from the sample are counted as a function of time.
Equation \eqref{eq:mdnp-dn} shows the delayed neutron counts as a function of time after irradiation.

\begin{equation}
    n_d(t) = \epsilon \sum_{i=1}^{I} P_{n_i} \lambda_i N_{i}(t)
    \label{eq:mdnp-dn}
\end{equation}

Many of the terms in Equation \eqref{eq:mdnp-dn} are the same as in \eqref{eq:dnpsingleeq}. The terms in Equation \eqref{eq:mdnp-dn} which are different are defined as follows:
\begin{align}
I &= \mbox{ the total number of \glspl{DNP} [-] } \nonumber \\
\epsilon &= \mbox{ the detector efficiency [-].} \nonumber
\end{align}

Once this delayed neutron temporal profile is generated, it is common to then use the data to generate group and kinetics parameters for that fissile nuclide and neutron energy.
The specifics of the methods used to form those parameters is discussed in Section \ref{sec:gandkparams}.

There are challenges associated with the collection of this experimental data \cite{bank2000jef}.
Experimental noise becomes more significant after long periods of time, as the delayed neutron counts decrease.
Additionally, the short lived groups cannot be measured directly due to moderated neutrons from irradiation.
This means that both the long and short time-scale measurements have greater uncertainty than the middle time-scale measurements.

Another consideration is measurement of the number of fissions.
Although the number of fission events can be measured somewhat accurately using a fission chamber after irradiation or other techniques, this does not account for source multiplication within the irradiated sample.
Keepin et al. suggests a technique to correct for the sample self-multiplication, which can cause differences of up to 3\% in the correction factor to the number of fissions.

\subsubsection{Microscopic Measurement of Delayed Neutron Precursors}

The other approach used to measure \glspl{DNP} is the microscopic approach, investigated in detail in the 1990-2000's \cite{pfeiffer2002status, d2002review, pfeiffer2002status, rudstam2002delayed, wilson2002delayed, brady1989delayed}, with progress through the 2010's via the \gls{IAEA} \gls{CRP} on Development of a Reference Database for
Beta-delayed neutron emission data \cite{mills2014calculation}. Leconte et al. used this approach as recently as 2020 \cite{leconte2020new}.
This approach entails taking a sample of a single \gls{DNP} nuclide and gathering experimental data on its properties.
Typically this involves some form of rapid chemical extraction or isotope separator and magnetic tape in order to rapidly measure short lived nuclides.

There are many different experimental methods used to study \glspl{DNP}, particularly their spectra: on-line mass spectrometry, helium-3 spectrometry, the proton-recoil technique, the time-of-flight method, and the neutron scintillator \cite{das1993importance}.
On-line mass spectrometry combined with a helium-3 ionization chamber and the proton-recoil technique allows for measurement of the delayed neutron energy spectrum \cite{shalev1972delayed, beyer2007proton}.
The time-of-flight method involves measuring the velocity of the delayed neutron by having a known travel distance and time measurement \cite{chulick1971energy, garcia2012monster, chrysochoides1971measurement}. An example of the time-of-flight experimental setup is shown in Figure \ref{fig:micromeasure}.

This microscopic measurement data is exceptionally useful, as it allows for treatment of \glspl{DNP} on a nuclide-by-nuclide basis, rather than macro-scale.
This is particularly important for \glspl{MSR}, where the spatial concentration of \glspl{DNP} depends on half-lives, yields, thermo-chemistry, and thermal-hydraulics.
With data on individual nuclides, higher fidelity treatment of \glspl{DNP} becomes possible.

There have been many new measurements in the past several decades on these nuclides.
Fortunately, the \gls{IAEA} has worked to compile and evaluate this data into a publicly accessible form \cite{DIMITRIOU2021144, dillmann2015summary}.
This data, when combined with cumulative fission yields and neutronics data, can be used to model the \glspl{DNP} explicitly, rather than using groups.
This is particularly useful for understanding what nuclides have the greatest impact as well as for simulating experimental configurations which have yet to be thoroughly investigated for \gls{DNP} group fitting.
