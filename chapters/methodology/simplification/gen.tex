The 0D flow model enables improved analysis of the general trends in nuclide evolutions.
It treats the flow in a zero-dimensional manner using in-core and ex-core residence times, and uses OpenMC to track the full depletion matrix.
Figure \ref{fig:msre1d} shows the 1D in-core and ex-core flow paths, which this 0D model emulates by irradiating the sample for the time it would be flowing through the in-core region and letting it decay for the time it would be flowing through the ex-core region.

The fission history function is a square wave, with a constant fission rate during the in-core residence time and no fission during the ex-core residence time.
This approximation simplifies the analytical form of the fission history for creating the non-linear least squares problem, as shown in Appendix \ref{ref:appendixA}.
Appendix \ref{ref:appendixA} derives Equation \eqref{eq:msr-group}, given here as:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{n}_{d}(T, t) =F_0 \sum_{k=1}^K \bar{\nu}_{d, k} e^{-\lambda_kt} \left( \left(J-J_{in}\right) - \frac{e^{-\lambda_kT}}{1-d}  \left( e^{\lambda_k\tau_{in}}d^{J_{in}+1} - e^{\lambda_k\tau_{in}} + 1 - d^{J+1} \right) \right),
    \label{eq:msr-group}
\end{equation}
where,
\begin{align}
    F_0 &= \text{the constant fission rate $[\# \cdot s^{-1}]$} \nonumber \\
    T &= \text{the total fission history time $[s]$} \nonumber \\
    J &= \left\lfloor \frac{T}{\tau_{net}} \right\rfloor \nonumber \\
    &= \text{the number of recirculations $[\#]$} \nonumber \\
    J_{in} &= \left\lfloor \frac{T-\tau_{in}}{\tau_{net}} \right\rfloor \nonumber \\
    &= \text{the number of recirculated in-core irradiations $[\#]$} \nonumber \\
    d &= e^{\lambda_k \tau_{net}}. \nonumber \\
\end{align}
However, this closed-form solution has numerical instabilities for very short ex-core irradiation times.
Although computationally slower to solve, Equation \eqref{eq:sumform} offers a more stable form, recreated here as:
\begin{align}
\frac{\dot{n}_{d, k}(T, t)}{F_0 \bar{\nu}_{d, k}} =& \sum_{j=0}^{J_{in}} \left( e^{-\lambda_k t} - e^{-\lambda_k (t + T - j \cdot \tau_{net})} - e^{-\lambda_k t} + e^{-\lambda_k (t + T - j \cdot \tau_{net} - \tau_{in})} \right) \nonumber \\ &+ \sum_{j=J_{in}+1}^{J} \left( e^{-\lambda_k t} - e^{-\lambda_k (t + T - j \cdot \tau_{net})} \right).
\end{align}

Figure \ref{fig:simplified-model} shows the flow diagram for a fission history to simulate irradiation on a fissile sample circulating in the primary loop.
The model passes eight parameters into OpenMC \cite{romano_openmc_2015}: in-core residence time, ex-core residence time, neutron irradiation energy, target fissile nuclide, target density, total irradiation time, temperature, and reprocessing terms.
The external data passed into OpenMC includes the emission probabilities and the depletion matrix.
The depletion matrix contains information on half-lives, fission yields, cross-sections, branching ratios, and other useful data needed for OpenMC depletion.
Section \ref{sec:delnu-counts} provides further details on this process.

After OpenMC completes the fixed-source simulation, the final nuclide concentrations in the irradiated sample are saved for analysis in \gls{MoSDeN}.
\gls{MoSDeN} combines the concentrations with data from the IAEA beta-delayed neutron emission database to generate a delayed neutron count rate \cite{DIMITRIOU2021144}.
A non-linear least squares fitting function from SciPy then calculates the group half-lives and yields using the Trust Region Reflective algorithm \cite{scipy, branch1999subspace}.
These group parameters correspond to a specific nuclide, neutron energy, in-core residence time, ex-core residence time, reprocessing scheme, and reprocessing scalar.
Section \ref{sec:delnu-groups} provides further details on this block.
Some additional factors to consider include self-shielding due to sample density (while avoiding self-multiplication), temperature-induced cross-section variations, and under- or over-irradiation, all of which can affect the results \cite{keepin1957delayed}.

The 0D flow model will use a three-gram spherical sample with vacuum boundary conditions.
It will treat the problem as a fixed-source simulation, with a uniform neutron source distributed evenly throughout the sphere.
This approach is not physical, as a real neutron source would have a spectrum and originate externally.
However, the exact neutron spectrum for irradiating samples to generate \gls{DNP} group parameters is not provided in the literature.
%The exact neutron energy spectrum is not needed, as only significant differences in the neutron energy spectrum affect the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
The source spatial distribution has negligible impact, as the sample is small and exhibits minimal self-multiplication.
Thus, this approximation does not limit the effectiveness of the 0D flow model.

Another consideration for the 0D flow model is maintaining a consistent source particle rate.
While not strictly necessary for group parameter generation since the values are per-fission, it simplifies the data analysis, as the tallies depend on the source particles.
Equation \eqref{eq:balancedsdot} provides the source rate for the irradiation simulations:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{S} = S_{net} \frac{\tau_{in} + \tau_{ex}}{\tau_{in} T},
    \label{eq:balancedsdot}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
\dot{S} &= \mbox{ the source particle rate [$\# \cdot s^{-1}$]} \nonumber \\ 
S_{net} &= \mbox{ the total number of source particles [$\#$]} \nonumber \\ 
\tau_{in} &= \mbox{ the in-core residence time [$s$]} \nonumber \\ 
\tau_{ex} &= \mbox{ the ex-core residence time [$s$]} \nonumber \\ 
T &= \mbox{ the net irradiation time [$s$].} \nonumber
\end{align}

After the OpenMC model, the custom scripts handle converting the concentrations into group parameters.
Within these scripts, the SciPy non-linear least squares fitting function is used to generate these group parameters.
However, there are some alterations included to ensure the fit is as good as possible.
The first is linearization and normalization of the data.
The normalization is handled by taking the delayed neutron counts, dividing by the fission term \footnote{The fission term is either the net number of fissions or the fission rate, depending on the irradiation type.}, and then taking the natural logarithm.
These normalized counts are then passed into the non-linear least squares fitting.
The linearization is handled by taking the natural logarithm of the entire delayed neutron count fit function.
This treatment reduces both the covariance and the $\chi^2$ of the resulting non-linear least squares fit.
Bounds are also set from zero to 1000 for each term to speed up the process, while the tolerances for convergence are set to $2.23 \times 10^{16}$ to ensure the converged solution is well-refined.

Uncertainties are tracked in this work using the uncertainties package \cite{uncertaintiespackage}.
Uncertainties in the concentrations generated from OpenMC are not accounted for, but uncertainties in the experimentally measured emission probabilities and half lives are.
These uncertainties are used in the minimization of $\chi^2$, shown in Equation \eqref{eq:chisq-nnls}.

\begin{equation}
    \chi^2 = \sum_{t=0}^{t_f} \left(  \frac{b_t - A_t x_t}{\sigma_t}  \right)
    \label{eq:chisq-nnls}
\end{equation}

The terms in Equation \eqref{eq:chisq-nnls} are defined as follows:

\begin{description}
    \item $t_f$ is the final time after irradiation, starting at $t=0$ immediately following irradiation
    \item $b_t$ is the delayed neutron count vector at the row where the time value is $t$
    \item $A_t$ is the coefficient matrix at the row where the time value is $t$
    \item $x_t$ is the solution vector at the row where the time value is $t$
\end{description}

\end{comment}
