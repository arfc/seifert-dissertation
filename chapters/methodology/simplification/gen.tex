To generate sufficient data for each combination of input parameters, a simplified model is necessary.
This simplified model will also provide estimates for the magnitude of variation which can be expected, as well as general trends.
Specifically, this model treats the flow in a zero dimensional manner by using in-core and ex-core residence times.
The fission history function is a square-wave, where the fission rate is some positive constant for the in-core residence time and zero for the ex-core residence time.
This approximation simplifies the analytical solve required to create the non-linear least-squares problem, as shown in Appendix \ref{ref:appendixA}.

Figure \ref{fig:simplified-model} shows how this fission history is used in OpenMC in order to mimic the irradiation on a control volume of fuel salt due to advection.
The model takes in eight parameters to pass into OpenMC \cite{romano_openmc_2015}: the in-core residence time, ex-core residence time, neutron irradiation energy, target fissile nuclide, target density, total irradiation time, temperature, and reprocessing terms.
Of these terms, three, shown in green, are not varied to generate new group parameters: the temperature, target density, and total irradiation time.
The other inputs are in yellow and vary to generate new group parameters.
More detail on this block is provided in Section \ref{sec:delnu-counts}.

After OpenMC finishes the fixed source simulation, the final concentrations of nuclides in the irradiated sample are saved for analysis in custom scripts.
These scripts take in the concentrations and combine them with the IAEA beta-delayed neutron emission database data to generate a delayed neutron count rate function \cite{DIMITRIOU2021144}.
A non-linear least squares fitting function from SciPy is then used to generate the half life and group abundances \cite{2020SciPy-NMeth}.
These group parameters are for a specific nuclide, neutron energy, in-core residence time, ex-core residence time, reprocessing scheme, and reprocessing scalar.
More detail on this block is provided in Section \ref{sec:delnu-groups}.

There are several considerations required for the OpenMC simulations.
The constant input parameters require some amount of consideration, which is discussed in Section \ref{sec:model-refinement}.
In particular, self-shielding of the sample due to density while avoiding self-multiplication, variations in cross sections due to temperature, and under or over irradiation can all affect the results and must be considered \cite{keepin1957delayed}.

The general form of the OpenMC model is a three gram spherical sample with vacuum boundary conditions.
This is treated as a fixed-source problem, with a uniform neutron source distributed evenly throughout the spherical sample.
This is not physical, as in reality the neutron source would have a spectrum and would be external.
However, for the 0D flow model, the desired effects to capture are the differences between the group parameters with flow and reprocessing compared to the static results.
This means such an approximation does not hinder the effectiveness of this 0D flow model.

Another consideration for the OpenMC model is having a consistent source particle rate.
This is not strictly necessary for group parameter generation, as the values are per-fission, but it is convenient for data analysis, as the tallies are based on the source particles.
The source rate for different simulations is given in Equation \eqref{eq:balancedsdot} as:

\begin{equation}
    \dot{S} = S_{net} \frac{\tau_{in} + \tau_{ex}}{\tau_{in} T},
    \label{eq:balancedsdot}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{align}
\dot{S} &= \mbox{ the source particle rate [$\frac{n}{s}$]} \nonumber \\ 
S_{net} &= \mbox{ the total number of source particles [$n$]} \nonumber \\ 
\tau_{in} &= \mbox{ the in-core residence time [$s$]} \nonumber \\ 
\tau_{ex} &= \mbox{ the ex-core residence time [$s$]} \nonumber \\ 
T &= \mbox{ the net irradiation time [$s$].} \nonumber
\end{align}

After the OpenMC model, the custom scripts handle converting the concentrations into group parameters.
Within these scripts, the SciPy non-linear least squares fitting function is used to generate these group parameters.
However, there are some alterations included to ensure the fit is as good as possible.
The first is linearization and normalization of the data.
The normalization is handled by taking the delayed neutron counts, dividing by the fission term \footnote{The fission term is either the net number of fissions or the fission rate, depending on the irradiation type.}, and then taking the natural logarithm.
These normalized counts are then passed into the non-linear least squares fitting.
The linearization is handled by taking the natural logarithm of the entire delayed neutron count fit function.
This treatment reduces both the covariance and the $\chi^2$ of the resulting non-linear least squares fit.
Bounds are also set from zero to 1000 for each term to speed up the process, while the tolerances for convergence are set to $2.23 \times 10^{16}$ to ensure the converged solution is well-refined.

Uncertainties are tracked in this work using the uncertainties package \cite{uncertaintiespackage}.
Uncertainties in the concentrations generated from OpenMC are not accounted for, but uncertainties in the experimentally measured emission probabilities and half lives are.
These uncertainties are used in the minimization of $\chi^2$, shown in Equation \eqref{eq:chisq-nnls}.

\begin{equation}
    \chi^2 = \sum_{t=0}^{t_f} \left(  \frac{b_t - A_t x_t}{\sigma_t}  \right)
    \label{eq:chisq-nnls}
\end{equation}

The terms in Equation \eqref{eq:chisq-nnls} are defined as follows:

\begin{description}
    \item $t_f$ is the final time after irradiation, starting at $t=0$ immediately following irradiation
    \item $b_t$ is the delayed neutron count vector at the row where the time value is $t$
    \item $A_t$ is the coefficient matrix at the row where the time value is $t$
    \item $x_t$ is the solution vector at the row where the time value is $t$
\end{description}

\end{comment}
