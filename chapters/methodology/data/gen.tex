This is the lowest fidelity of the three models, and it primarily serves as a way to verify the microscopic approach is working as expected by comparing with existing literature.
This approach can only model saturation concentrations, as there is no decay chain tracking nuclides decaying into each other.
It is called the 0D scaled model because it uses the scaled flux method, discussed in Section \ref{sec:bateman}.
The cumulative fission yield is used to calculate the equilibrium \gls{DNP} concentrations, with a scaling term based on the amount of fuel salt in the core.
An example solution with no reprocessing is shown in Equation \eqref{eq:cfy-conc} as \cite{huynh2014calculation}:
\begin{equation}
    N_i = \frac{f_{in} CFY_i}{\lambda_i},
    \label{eq:cfy-conc}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    f_{in} &= \text{the fraction of fuel-salt in the core} \nonumber.
\end{align}
The derivation for this equation is given in Appendix \ref{sec:appendixB}.
It is important to note that the flux is scaled when calculating the fission rate as well, by the same $f_{in}$ term that scales the cumulative fission yield.
Effectively, this cancels the term out, resulting in no deviation in the results based on the in-core fuel salt fraction by reprocessing time.

No parasitic absorption is accounted for in this model, nor is time dependent decay of nuclides into \glspl{DNP}.
There is a possible approach to include the effects of parasitic absorption, but this approach is not included \cite{shahbazi_steady-state_2022, benedictnuclear}.

The blue squares represent the outputs, in this case the \gls{DNP} group parameters: the \gls{DNP} group yields and the \gls{DNP} group half-lives.

After all this data is loaded into \gls{MoSDeN}, it generates the concentration of each \gls{DNP}.
This concentration is then combined with emission probabilities and decay constants to form a delayed neutron count rate.
A non-linear least squares solve then fits the improved \gls{DNP} group parameters to the delayed neutron count rate, generating the group yields and half-lives, given as blue squares in Figure \ref{fig:data-model}.

This model is only valid for saturation irradiation simulations, as the concentration calculated in Equation \eqref{eq:cfy-conc} is the equilibrium concentration.
For a pulse irradiation, this approach is not valid.
There are four different options available to handle pulse irradiations: reduce net decay measurement time, pull only the shortest lived group(s) with independent fission yields, use OpenMC depletion, or simply do not use the shortest lived groups typically generated from a pulse irradiation since they are unlikely to be changed by \gls{MSR} physical phenomena.
The first approach, reducing the net decay time, will result in overfitting of the short lived groups, leading to less accurate results.
The second approach, using only the shortest lived groups with independent fission yields means that the decay chains will not be handled, but the initial pulse concentration should be accurate.
However, the concentrations will quickly become less accurate as the decays of various nuclides into \glspl{DNP} will not be treated, so this approach could only be potentially viable for the shortest lived group.
The third approach, using OpenMC depletion, would be accurate and easily achievable.
However, it would greatly increase computational cost, and the use of OpenMC in such a case is already handled by the 0D flow model, making it redundant.
The final approach would entail only using the longer lived \gls{DNP} group parameters from a saturation irradiation, while recognizing that the short lived groups are less accurate.
Because the \gls{MSR} physical phenomena that cause non-negligible influence on the \glspl{DNP} take at least several seconds to come into effect, it is reasonable to focus on the longer lived groups.
