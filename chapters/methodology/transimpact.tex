The impact of the \gls{DNP} groups on transients will be evaluated using Moltres with fluid-flow-velocity field coupled multi-group diffusion kinetics, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:mgd-kinetics}.
This approach provides high-fidelity results by coupling thermal-hydraulics and neutronics, a necessity for \gls{MSR} modeling.
Modeling every \gls{DNP} in space and time would be prohibitively expensive, so they are instead modeled using \gls{DNP} group parameters.
Moltres allows direct comparison between static \gls{DNP} group parameters, which do not account for \gls{MSR} phenomena, and parameters that do.

The results of interest from this analysis include the reactivity and power responses to various initiating events.
The initiating events and transients vary, as different reactor responses are studied.
The events of interest depend on the perspective from which the reactor response is analyzed.

Specifically, there are four different perspectives of interest in this work: validation, safety, security, and safeguards.
Each perspective involves a unique combination of motivation, proposed initiating event, and investigated response.
These are discussed in detail in their respective sections.

The effective delayed neutron fraction $\beta_{eff}$ is also of interest and will be calculated.
It can be calculated from Moltres using the prompt method, discussed in Section \ref{sec:calc-kinetic-param}.
Moltres uses fission-weighted \gls{DNP} group parameters, and the effective multiplication factor will capture the spatial distribution of each \gls{DNP} group concentration.
Adjoint-weighting is not used for these parameters because Moltres explicitly captures the delayed neutrons in the diffusion solve.
The effective delayed neutron fraction will be compared between the improved \gls{DNP} groups and existing groups to offer more detail for comparison between the groups.

Moltres will provide an effective delayed neutron fraction that captures the effects of fuel circulation.
The fission weighting values will be generated using fission rate tallies in OpenMC.

\subsection{Validation}
The motivation for validation is to confirm that the proposed methodology and results align with experimental data.
This ensures the demonstrated improved \gls{DNP} group parameters are applicable to actual \glspl{MSR}.
Discrepancies between experimental results and the model highlight potential weaknesses in the approach.
If no discrepancies exist, confidence in the results increases for problems similar to those validated.

The validation of the \gls{DNP} group parameters follows a multi-stage approach.
The first stage compares the irradiated sample’s delayed neutron count rate to the Keepin et al. experiment from 1957 \cite{keepin1957delayed}.
If the count rates do not match, the issue lies in the data or the aggregation of individual \glspl{DNP} into the count rate.
If the count rate is accurately predicted, the formulation is considered valid.
The second stage validates the improved \gls{DNP} group parameters using \gls{MSRE} pump start-up and coast-down experimental data to assess their accuracy in modeling reactivity changes from \glspl{DNP} \cite{prince1968zero}.
This experiment has been studied using Moltres and Quasimolto\footnote{Quasimolto is an open-source multiphysics code that models multi-group neutron transport, \gls{DNP} balance, and energy balance.}, allowing comparison with their results \cite{reynolds2023analysis, park_advancements_2025}.
However, previous models excluded the inlet and outlet plena in the \gls{MSRE}, leading to discrepancies with experimental data.
A model including the plena, combined with updated \gls{DNP} group parameters, should yield results closer to the experimental data.

\subsection{Safety}
The motivation for studying safety is to protect the public and meet regulatory requirements.
Safety is discussed in detail in Section \ref{sec:safety}, though the general takeaway is that many safety-related transients exist.
The \gls{DNP} group parameters are critical in these transients, as they influence the reactor period, power, and temperature.

The transients that will be investigated are \gls{ULOHS}, \gls{ULOF}, \gls{UTOP}, and \gls{FSOC}.
Each transient will be simulated using Moltres in the \gls{MSRE} using existing \gls{DNP} groups and the improved \gls{DNP} groups.
For each transient, the power response, maximum temperature, minimum temperature, and reactivity response will be recorded.
The power response shows how changes in the \gls{DNP} group parameters affect other responses, such as temperature.
It is important to make sure differences in the \gls{DNP} parameters don't lead to temperature changes or oscillations which result in unsafe reactor conditions.

\subsection{Security}
Security is discussed in detail in Section \ref{sec:security}, but the general takeaway is that bad actors may attempt to sabotage the reactor.
This ties directly into the safety design study, as sabotage aims to create unsafe reactor conditions.
Ensuring the \gls{MSR} operates safely after sabotage is critical.
If static \gls{DNP} group parameters predict safe operation while improved parameters predict unsafe operation, correcting this discrepancy in the reactor design is essential.

Since the safety study covers most transients relevant to sabotage, additional considerations can be incorporated into these transients.
As sabotage may initiate multiple safety-related transients simultaneously, it is reasonable to investigate such scenarios.
With four unique transients, this results in six double-transient combinations, three triple-transient combinations, and one quadruple-transient combination, totaling ten security-related sabotage-initiated transients.
Each of these transients will be simulated using Moltres in the \gls{MSRE} using existing \gls{DNP} groups and the improved \gls{DNP} groups.

\subsection{Safeguards}
\label{sec:safeguards-trans}
Safeguards are discussed in Section \ref{sec:safeguards}, but the main takeaway is the importance of preventing \gls{SNM} diversion in \glspl{MSR}.
Detection methods, such as neutron detectors and surveillance systems, play a key role in safeguarding the reactor \cite{zubair2024nuclear}.
The improved \gls{DNP} group parameters in this work affect the effective delayed neutron fraction and reactivity response.
Using static \gls{DNP} group parameters to detect \gls{SNM} diversion predicts less accurate reactivity responses than the improved parameters.
If the difference is significant, relying on static parameters may reduce the detection probability of a diversion scenario.

To investigate this, diversion scenarios will be studied for a single significant quantity of $^{235}$U and plutonium removed over the course of one year, simulated using OpenMC.
The diversion will be from the \gls{MSRE} from a fresh fuel salt over the course of one year at nominal power. 
The change in the fuel salt composition will affect the \gls{DNP} group parameters, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:calc-group-param}.
The fission-weighted \gls{DNP} group parameters can be used in Moltres, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:moltres}, for simple step reactivity insertion transients.
The following four sets of fission-weighted \gls{DNP} group parameters will be compared: existing parameters without diversion, existing parameters with diversion, improved parameters without diversion, and improved parameters with diversion.
A simple step reactivity insertion will be simulated for each set of \gls{DNP} group parameters, from which the power responses can be compared.

The power responses will demonstrate if power response fingerprinting could be obfuscated if an \gls{MSR} transient does not use improved \gls{DNP} groups.
If the existing \gls{DNP} group parameters with diversion is within uncertainty of no diversion with improved \gls{DNP} group parameters, then there may be false positives if transient simulations do not use improved \gls{DNP} groups.
Using the improved \gls{DNP} groups would reduce this false positive rate by accounting for the recirculation and chemical removal, improving the accuracy of using power response to identify material diversion.
