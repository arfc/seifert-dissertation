The impact of the \gls{DNP} groups on transients will be evaluated using Moltres with fluid-flow-velocity field coupled multi-group diffusion kinetics, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:bg-trans-analysis-methods}.
This approach provides high-fidelity results by coupling thermal-hydraulics and neutronics, a necessity for \gls{MSR} modeling.
Modeling every \gls{DNP} in space and time would be prohibitively expensive, so they are instead modeled using \gls{DNP} group parameters.
Moltres allows direct comparison between static \gls{DNP} group parameters, which do not account for \gls{MSR} phenomena, and parameters that do.
This comparison will be performed by varying the \gls{DNP} group parameters in Moltres and simulating the same transient, followed by comparison of temperature and power responses.

The results of interest from this analysis include the reactivity and power responses to various initiating events.
The initiating events and transients vary, as different reactor responses are studied.
The events of interest depend on the analytical perspective.
Specifically, there are four different perspectives of interest in this work: validation, safety, security, and safeguards.
Each perspective involves a unique combination of motivation, proposed initiating event, and investigated response.
These are discussed in detail in their respective sections.

The effective delayed neutron fraction $\beta_{eff}$ is also of interest and will be calculated.
It can be calculated from Moltres using the prompt method, discussed in Section \ref{sec:gandkparams}.
Moltres uses fission-weighted \gls{DNP} group parameters, and the effective multiplication factor will capture the spatial distribution of each \gls{DNP} group concentration.
Adjoint-weighting is not used for these parameters because Moltres explicitly captures the delayed neutrons in the diffusion solve.
The effective delayed neutron fraction will be compared between the improved \gls{DNP} groups and existing groups to offer more detail for comparison between the groups.

Moltres will provide an effective delayed neutron fraction that captures the effects of fuel circulation.
The fission weighting values will be generated using fission rate tallies in OpenMC.

\subsection{Validation}
The motivation for validation is to confirm that the proposed methodology and results align with experimental data.
This ensures that the demonstrated improved \gls{DNP} group parameters are applicable to actual \glspl{MSR}.
Discrepancies between experimental results and the model highlight potential weaknesses in the approach.
If no discrepancies exist, confidence in the results increases for problems similar to those validated.

In this work, the validation of the \gls{DNP} group parameters will follow a multi-stage approach.
%The first stage will compare the irradiated sample’s delayed neutron count rate to the Keepin et al. experiment from 1957 \cite{keepin1957delayed}.
The first stage will compare the irradiated sample's \gls{DNP} group parameters from thermal fission of $^{235}$U with no ex-core time to the values in the literature \cite{keepin1957delayed, charlton1996delayed, endf6, mills1991study, brady1989delayed, saleh1997measurements, synetos1983delayed, tuttle1975delayed, waldo1981delayed, parish1999status}.
This comparison will demonstrate that the model accurately aligns with experimentally measured values for a stationary case.
If the parameters do not match, the issue lies in the data or the aggregation of individual \glspl{DNP} into the count rate.
If the parameters are accurately predicted, the formulation is considered valid.

The second stage of the validation will use the \gls{MSRE} modeled in Moltres to compare the effective delayed neutron fraction.
The literature reports the effective delayed neutron fraction reduced from 0.0045 by 230 to 304 $pcm$, which will be compared to values calculated using the previous and improved \gls{DNP} group parameters \cite{haubenreich1970experience, shi2021gen, prince1968zero}.
%The circulation also led to a change in reactivity, specifically a 210 $pcm$ negative reactivity insertion from delayed neutron circulation, with 20 $pcm$ or less from gas bubble entrainment \cite{haubenreich1970experience}.
%The effective delayed neutron fraction was 0.0045 as the $^{235}$U fuel circulated in the \gls{MSRE} \cite{haubenreich1970experience}.
This stage will require OpenMC k-eigenvalue calculations to generate multi-group cross section data to pass into Moltres using a fresh fuel salt.
This will enable generation of two sets of \gls{DNP} groups: a conventional and an improved set of \gls{DNP} group parameters.
These results will then be compared with \gls{DNP} groups in the literature for the \gls{MSRE} to determine which set of parameters most closely fits the measured effective delayed neutron fraction \cite{lee2022transient}.

The third stage of the validation that will be conducted in this work applies the improved \gls{DNP} group parameters to the \gls{MSRE} pump start-up and coast-down transient simulation to compare against experimental data on reactivity changes from \glspl{DNP} \cite{prince1968zero}.
The purpose of this comparison is to demonstrate that the non-stationary model generates valid \gls{DNP} group parameters.
This experiment was previously studied by Reynolds and Palmer, as well as by Park using Moltres and Quasimolto\footnote{Quasimolto is an open-source multiphysics code that models multi-group neutron transport, \gls{DNP} balance, and energy balance.}, allowing comparison with their results \cite{reynolds2023analysis, park_advancements_2025}.
However, previous models excluded the inlet and outlet plena in the \gls{MSRE}, leading to discrepancies with experimental data.
This work will develop a model including the plena, to be combined with updated \gls{DNP} group parameters, to generate delayed neutron reactivity responses closer to the experimental data.


\subsection{Safety}
The motivation for studying safety is to protect the public and meet regulatory requirements.
Safety is discussed in detail in Section \ref{sec:safety}, which showed that many safety-related transients exist.
The \gls{DNP} group parameters are critical in these transients, as they influence the reactor period, power, and temperature.

The transients that will be investigated in the \gls{MSRE} are the \gls{ULOHS}, \gls{ULOF}, \gls{UTOP}, and \gls{FSOC} events \cite{li2015transient, daher2022improvement}.
A \gls{ULOHS} transient can occur when the heat exchanger experiences a failure, possibly triggered by a pump failure in the intermediate loop, leading to a loss of cooling of the reactor.
This will be modeled in Moltres by changing the inlet temperature to match the outlet temperature, rather than using a constant inlet temperature.
This will simulate a loss of heat sink, as rather than the inlet temperature being constant and lower than the outlet temperature, it will simulate no cooling.
A {ULOF} transient is when the flow rate in the primary loop becomes zero, caused by pump failure in the primary loop.
This will be modeled in Moltres by slowing the velocity field, which will alter both the temperature and \gls{DNP} distribution.
A \gls{UTOP} transient is a reactivity insertion event, which could be caused by various means, such as removing poisons, adding fissile material, or temperature changes.
This will be modeled in Moltres by having a step decrease in the removal cross section, which will represent a control rod withdrawal.
An \gls{FSOC} transient occurs when the fuel salt temperature decreases, which could be caused by an increased heat removal rate from the heat exchanger.
This will be modeled in Moltres by decreasing the inlet fuel salt temperature.

Each transient will be simulated using Moltres in the \gls{MSRE} using existing \gls{DNP} groups and the improved \gls{DNP} groups.
For each transient, the power response, maximum temperature, minimum temperature, and reactivity response will be recorded.
The power response shows how changes in the \gls{DNP} group parameters affect other responses, such as temperature.
It is important to evaluate if \glspl{MSR} may have unexpected unsafe temperatures.
This could occur if traditional \gls{DNP} group parameters predict safe operation while the improved parameters predict unsafe operation.

\subsection{Security}
Security is discussed in detail in Section \ref{sec:security}, which details that, for this work, scenarios of interest are when actors use an insider to disrupt the reactor through sabotage.
This ties directly into the safety design study, as sabotage aims to create unsafe reactor conditions.
Ensuring the \gls{MSR} operates safely after sabotage is critical.
If static \gls{DNP} group parameters predict safe operation while improved parameters predict unsafe operation, correcting this discrepancy in the reactor design is essential.

Since the safety study covers most transients relevant to sabotage, additional considerations can be incorporated into these transients.
As sabotage may initiate multiple safety-related transients simultaneously, it is reasonable to investigate such scenarios.
Specifically, the attack scenario assumes that multiple simultaneous events are instantiated by an insider in combination with a hostile actor \cite{iaea_national_2021}.
This aligns with specifications provided in 10 CFR 73.1 on a design basis threat, which includes an internal threat for radiological sabotage as a diversionary action \cite{CFR2022}.
The radiological sabotage design basis threat includes well-trained, armed, and informed individuals with insider assistance, an internal threat, and a cyber attack.

The four unique transients that will be considered are the \gls{ULOHS}, \gls{ULOF}, \gls{UTOP}, and \gls{FSOC}.
Combining them to form all unique combinations results in six double-transients, three triple-transients, and one quadruple-transient, totaling ten combined security-related sabotage-initiated transients.
However, some of these combinations do not result in a change, such as the \gls{ULOF} and \gls{ULOHS}, as the loss of heat sink would not be important for a stationary fuel salt that does not circulate out of the core to the heat exchanger.
The combinations that will be considered are each transient on its own and the \gls{UTOP} combined with each other transient.
This results in four single transients and three double transients, totaling seven transient simulations that will be analyzed.
It is feasible that the attack scenario for the radiological sabotage design basis threat could cause these seven transients in an attempt to disrupt operations and divert attention.
Each of these transients will be simulated using Moltres in the \gls{MSRE} using existing \gls{DNP} groups and the improved \gls{DNP} groups.

These security analyses will demonstrate if certain combinations of transients predicted to be safe and non-disruptive with previous \gls{DNP} groups may actually be disruptive when using the improved \gls{DNP} groups.
The transient will be considered disruptive if safety limits on temperature are exceeded.
If there are sabotage events which are predicted to be non-disruptive by the previous groups but not by improved groups, then this would indicate an area of improvement for modeling and reactor design.
If the improved \gls{DNP} groups do not demonstrate disruptive effects, then this analysis will still provide useful insight into how much variation in temperature could be expected due to different transient combinations.

\subsection{Safeguards}
\label{sec:safeguards-trans}
Safeguards are discussed in Section \ref{sec:safeguards}, with a focus on the importance of preventing \gls{SNM} diversion in \glspl{MSR}.
Detection methods, such as neutron detectors and surveillance systems, play a key role in safeguarding the reactor \cite{zubair2024nuclear}.
The improved \gls{DNP} group parameters in this work affect the effective delayed neutron fraction and reactivity response.
Using static \gls{DNP} group parameters to detect \gls{SNM} diversion predicts less accurate reactivity responses than the improved parameters.
If the difference is significant, relying on static parameters may reduce the detection probability of a diversion scenario.

To investigate this, diversion scenarios will be studied for a single significant quantity of $^{235}$U and another scenario of a single significant quantity of plutonium removed over the course of one year, simulated using OpenMC.
The diversion will be from the $^{235}$U-fueled \gls{MSRE} from a fresh fuel salt over the course of one year at nominal power. 
The change in the fuel salt composition will affect the \gls{DNP} group parameters, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:gandkparams}.
The fission-weighted \gls{DNP} group parameters can be used in Moltres, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:moltres}, for simple step reactivity insertion transients.
The following four sets of fission-weighted \gls{DNP} group parameters will be compared: existing parameters without diversion, existing parameters with diversion, improved parameters without diversion, and improved parameters with diversion.
The power responses will be compared using a simple negative step reactivity insertion will be simulated for each set of \gls{DNP} group parameters.

The power responses will demonstrate if power response fingerprinting could be obfuscated for an \gls{MSR} model that does not use improved \gls{DNP} groups.
This obfuscation will be investigated by comparing power responses, which are dependent on the delayed neutron fraction.
The delayed neutron yield, and therefore the delayed neutron fraction, will be smaller when accounting for chemical removal and recirculation, which will also be true for measured responses in the \gls{MSR}.
This smaller delayed neutron fraction will cause a larger power response, which will more accurately reflect what would be measured in an \gls{MSR}.
A model using existing \gls{DNP} groups would have a larger delayed neutron fraction than it should, which would lead to a smaller than expected power response.

If there is a diversion of uranium, then there will be more plutonium fission, which will decrease the effective delayed neutron fraction.
This will lead to a larger power response.
The opposite is true for a $^{235}$U diversion, which will lead to a smaller power response.

Based on these effects, a case with no diversion while using existing \gls{DNP} group parameters would predict a smaller power response than the reactor will measure.
This will increase the false-positive detection probability for $^{235}$U diversion.
Additionally, a plutonium diversion would lead to the measured reactor power response aligning with the response using existing \gls{DNP} groups. 
By using the improved \gls{DNP} group parameters, this difference between the modeled and actual delayed neutron fraction can be reduced, allowing the power response to more accurately fingerprint diversion of nuclear material.
