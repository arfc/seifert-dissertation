
Because the microscopic approach uses individual \glspl{DNP}, it becomes possible to analyze the contribution of each \gls{DNP} to the total delayed neutron yield.
This is given in Equation \eqref{eq:delnu-net-nuc-CFY}, recreated here as:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\nu}_d = \sum_{i=1}^I P_{n, i} CFY_i. \nonumber
\end{equation}
Another quantity of interest is the number of delayed neutrons from the existing concentration of nuclides, rather than the number of delayed neutrons per fission.
This can be calculated by integrating the delayed neutron count rate, as shown in Equation \eqref{eq:delnu-net-nuc}, recreated here as:
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^{\infty} \dot{n}_{d, i}(t) dt= P_{n, i} N_{i} (0). \nonumber
\end{equation}

Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions} shows both of these approaches and how they differ.
Figure \ref{fig:delnu-counts-stacked-rates}(a) shows the delayed neutron yield contributions, which indicate the \glspl{DNP} which emit the most delayed neutrons after a single fission event.
Figure \ref{fig:delnu-counts-stacked-rates}(b) shows the contribution of each \glspl{DNP} to the delayed neutron count rate for a saturation irradiation.
This does not show the delayed neutron yield, but contributes directly to the \gls{DNP} group yields, as the delayed neutron count rate is critical for calculating the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
Figure \ref{fig:delnu-counts-stacked-rates}(c) shows the concentrations of each \gls{DNP}, which is correlated to Figure \ref{fig:delnu-counts-stacked-rates}(b).
\Glspl{DNP} with a large concentration may have a small emission probability and not contribute many delayed neutrons to the count rate, such as $^{141}$Cs.

For the delayed neutron yield contributions, shown in Figure \ref{fig:delnu-counts-stacked-rates}(a), $^{86}$Ge has the largest contribution to the delayed neutron yield, which means that for each thermal fission event in $^{235}$U, approximately 12.5\% of the delayed neutrons come from $^{86}$Ge.
$^{86}$Ge is very short lived, with a half-life of approximately 200 milliseconds.
This is why, in Figure \ref{fig:delnu-counts-stacked-rates}(b), it contributes less than 4\% of the total delayed neutrons once the sample has been irradiated for some time.
Since it has been irradiated, the longer lived nuclides have accumulated, and the delayed neutron count rate is dominated by them.
This reinforces the justification of why pulse irradiation data is important, as the saturation irradiation does not fully capture the relative effects of the short-lived \glspl{DNP}.
Finally, the relative concentration of each \gls{DNP} is shown in Figure \ref{fig:delnu-counts-stacked-rates}(c).
The concentrations are generally consistent with the literature, though $^{141}$Cs is an outlier \cite{leconte2020new}.
This may be due to the simplifications made in the 0D scaled model, such as exclusion of parasitic absorption, or that other works ignore $^{141}$Cs due to its exceedingly small emission probability.
