
Because the microscopic approach uses individual \glspl{DNP}, it is possible to analyze the contribution of each \gls{DNP} to the total delayed neutron yield.
This is given in Equation \eqref{eq:delnu-net-nuc-CFY}, recreated here as:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\nu}_d = \sum_{i=1}^I P_{n, i} CFY_i. \nonumber
\end{equation}
Another quantity of interest is the number of delayed neutrons from the existing concentration of nuclides, rather than the number of delayed neutrons per fission.
This can be calculated by integrating the delayed neutron count rate, as shown in Equation \eqref{eq:delnu-net-nuc}, recreated here as:
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^{\infty} \dot{n}_{d, i}(t) dt= P_{n, i} N_{i} (0). \nonumber
\end{equation}

Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions} shows both approaches and how they differ.
Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(a) shows the delayed neutron yield contributions, which indicate the \glspl{DNP} which emit the most delayed neutrons after a single fission event.
Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(b) shows each \glspl{DNP}' contribution to the delayed neutron count rate for a saturation irradiation.
The count rate contribution is weighed more heavily towards longer-lived \glspl{DNP} because they build up during the saturation irradiation.
Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(c) shows the concentrations of each \gls{DNP}, correlated with Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(b).
\Glspl{DNP} with a large concentration may have a small emission probability and contribute few delayed neutrons to the count rate, such as $^{141}$Cs.

For the delayed neutron yield contributions in Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(a), $^{86}$Ge contributes the most, meaning that for each thermal fission event in $^{235}$U, approximately 12.5\% of delayed neutrons come from $^{86}$Ge.
$^{86}$Ge is exceedingly short-lived, with a half-life of approximately 200 milliseconds.
In Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(b), it contributes less than 4\% of the total delayed neutrons after the sample has been irradiated.
Longer-lived \glspl{DNP}, such as $^{137}$I, accumulate during irradiation, dominating the delayed neutron count rate.
This reinforces why pulse irradiation data are important, as saturation irradiation does not fully capture short-lived \glspl{DNP} effects.
Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(c) shows the relative concentration of each \gls{DNP}.
The concentrations are generally consistent with the literature, though $^{141}$Cs is an outlier \cite{leconte2020new}.
This may result from the 0D scaled model simplifications, such as excluding parasitic absorption, or because other works ignore $^{141}$Cs due to its extremely small emission probability.
