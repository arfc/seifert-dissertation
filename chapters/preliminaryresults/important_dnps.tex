
As discussed in Section \ref{sec:measurementofdnps}, the microscopic approach uses individual \glspl{DNP} to generate \gls{DNP} group parameters.
Because of this, the contribution of each \gls{DNP} to the total delayed neutron yield can be evaluated..
This is given in Equation \eqref{eq:delnu-net-nuc-CFY}:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\nu}_d = \sum_{i=1}^I P_{n, i} CFY_i. \nonumber
\end{equation}
The total delayed neutron yield is a quantity of interest, as it is used for calculation of the delayed neutron fraction.

Another quantity of interest is the number of delayed neutrons from the existing concentration of nuclides, rather than the yield per fission.
This quantity is particularly useful for saturation irradiations, where concentrations of different \glspl{DNP} will accumulate over time when compared to the quantities present for a single fission event.
This can be calculated by integrating the delayed neutron count rate, as shown in Equation \eqref{eq:delnu-net-nuc}, recreated here as:
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^{\infty} \dot{n}_{d, i}(t) dt= P_{n, i} N_{i} (0). \nonumber
\end{equation}

Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions} shows both approaches and how they differ.
Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(a) shows the delayed neutron yield contributions from each \gls{DNP}, which indicate the \glspl{DNP} which emit the most delayed neutrons after a single fission event.
Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(b) shows each \glspl{DNP}' contribution to the delayed neutron count rate for a saturation irradiation.
The count rate contribution is weighted more heavily towards longer-lived \glspl{DNP} because they build up during the saturation irradiation.
Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(c) shows the concentrations of each \gls{DNP}, correlated with Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(b) as shown in Equation \eqref{eq:delnu-net-nuc}.
\Glspl{DNP} with a large concentration may have a small emission probability and contribute few delayed neutrons to the count rate, such as $^{141}$Cs.

For the delayed neutron yield contributions in Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(a), $^{86}$Ge contributes the most, meaning that for each thermal fission event in $^{235}$U, approximately 12.5\% of delayed neutrons come from $^{86}$Ge.
$^{86}$Ge is exceedingly short-lived, with a half-life of approximately 200 milliseconds.
In Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(b), it contributes less than 4\% of the total delayed neutrons after the sample has been irradiated.
Longer-lived \glspl{DNP}, such as $^{137}$I, accumulate during irradiation, dominating the delayed neutron count rate.
This helps explain why pulse irradiation data are important in experimental measurements, as saturation irradiations are dominated by long-lived \glspl{DNP}.
Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}(c) shows the relative concentration of each \gls{DNP}.
The concentrations are generally consistent with the literature, though $^{141}$Cs appears to be an outlier \cite{leconte2020new}.
This may result from the 0D scaled model simplifications, such as excluding parasitic absorption, or because other works ignore $^{141}$Cs due to its extremely small emission probability.
