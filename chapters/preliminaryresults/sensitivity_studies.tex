
This section covers the sensitivity of the \gls{DNP} group parameters to various simulation parameters.
The simulation parameters evaluated are the chemical removal, the time nodes at which to evaluate the non-linear least squares problem, and the uncertainties in the nuclear data.
For the chemical removal analysis, the \gls{MSBR} chemical reprocessing scheme is used.
The \gls{MSBR} reprocessing scheme has large chemical removal rates, providing a conservative impact of these chemical removal rates on the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
The evaluation of the time nodes include the spacing between each node, the number of nodes, the total amount of time the sample should decay, and a combination of the total time and number of nodes.
Finally, the data uncertainties include uncertainty in the fission yields, half-lives, and emission probabilities.
To propagate these uncertainties to the \gls{DNP} group parameters, the non-linear least squares solver is run 5000 times with a random normal distribution sample taken from the fission yield, half-life, and emission probability of each \gls{DNP}.

\subsection{Chemical Removal}

As shown in Appendix \ref{sec:appendixB}, the fraction of fuel salt in the ex-core region determines the scaling of the chemical removal rate.
For this analysis, the ex-core fraction will be assumed to be 0.5.
This value is based on the approximate core zone I, core zone II, and annulus salt volume relative to the primary system salt volume in the \gls{MSBR} \cite{robertson_conceptual_1971}.
The chemical reprocessing scheme from the \gls{MSBR} used in this analysis is given in Table \ref{tab:msbr_cycle_times}, recreated here.

One consideration is that the 0D scaled model will underestimate the effect of the chemical removal rate.
This is because it only chemically removes from the cumulative fission yield of the element in the reprocessing scheme, and does not account for decay chains which may have chemical removal rates included.
For example, $^{137}$I has a cycle time of 60 days, or a chemical removal rate of $1.9\times 10^{-7}$ $[s^{-1}]$.
However, Sb and Te, which both decay into I, also have a chemical removal rate of 0.05 $[s^{-1}]$, many orders of magnitudes larger than the I removal rate.
However, using the 0D scaled model, with cumulative fission yields and no decay chain, means that this effect is not captured.

\subsection{Time Nodes}
\label{sec:time-eval}
The time node sensitivity is important, as many time nodes may be required to capture the physics fully, but at an increasing cost for each additional time node.
Of interest are the distribution of nodes, the number of nodes, the amount of time the sample decays, and the combined decay time and number of nodes.

\subsubsection{Distribution}
For the distribution of time nodes, two different approaches are compared: a logarithmic spacing between 0.01 and 600 seconds, and a linear spacing between 0 and 600 seconds.
Because 300 time nodes are being used, this means that the linear spacing will not capture behavior which occurs in the first 2 seconds, but instead will have a higher resolution at the longer time scales.
This may not be as useful, as at longer time scales the number of \glspl{DNP} that are relevant drop away quickly, as discussed in Section  \ref{sec:important-dnp}.

Figure \ref{fig:time-distribution} shows how the \gls{DNP} group parameters compare between the logarithmic and linear spacing.
The half-lives of the shorter lived groups are measured as being larger for the linear spacing, which is consistent with expectations.
The linear spacing does not have any data between zero and two seconds, and there is greater weighting on the longer lived groups due to the data distribution.
This also leads to the shorter lived groups capturing a larger yield, with the yields being pulled from groups two, three, and four to be added to groups five and six.
The difference between the total \gls{DNP} yields is approximately 0.1\%, so changing the spacing of the time nodes does not change the macroscopic behavior of the \gls{DNP} groups.
Instead, it affects the distribution of delayed neutrons over time, where the logarithmic distribution is more accurate due to capturing the effects of shorter lived \glspl{DNP}.

\subsubsection{Total Decay Time}
\label{sec:total-decay-time}
As the total decay time increases, two effects occur at the same time: a decrease in the time node density, discussed in Section \ref{sec:time-tot-and-node}, and an increase in the number of nodes evaluated at longer times.
Because the longest lived \gls{DNP}, $^{87}$Br, has a half-life of approximately one minute, it is reasonable to assume that the default final time of ten minutes offers sufficient data on all \gls{DNP} decays.

Figure \ref{fig:time-tot-dec-time} shows that this is true.
The differences in \gls{DNP} group parameters between a 600 second decay time and a 1000 second decay time are very small relative to the uncertainty bounds.
The short decay times do not accurately model the \glspl{DNP}, with underestimations of the half-lives.
The half-life underestimations are then correlated with overestimation of some of the longer lived \gls{DNP} group yields.
The \gls{DNP} yield of groups two and four have an interesting trend, where they underestimate for short decay times and overestimate for intermediate decay times.
This is likely due to the simultaneous fitting of the surrounding groups, as the raw data itself remains unchanged.

\subsection{Nuclear Data Uncertainties}
To find the the sensitivity of the \gls{DNP} group parameters to the uncertainties in the nuclear data, a Monte Carlo approach is used.
From each piece of data used in the 0D scaled model, such as the half-life of $^{87}$Br, a random value is sampled assuming a normal distribution.
This is repeated for every \glspl{DNP}' fission yield, half-life, and emission probability.

\subsubsection{Count Rate}
The count rate, normalized to the Keepin et al. count rate, is given in Figure \ref{fig:keepin-counts-0dscaled}.
The red circles indicate the count rates from various sampled simulations, while the blue dashed line indicates the \gls{DNP} group parameters used and the uncertainty based on the 1000 Monte Carlo sampled non-linear least squares solves.
The black X's indicate the mean count rate at each point.
The other six group \gls{DNP} parameters plotted include Keepin, which is 1.0 since the plot is normalized to the Keepin values, the Brady group parameters, and the Synetos group parameters \cite{keepin1957delayed, brady1989delayed, synetos1983delayed}.

This figure shows that beyond roughly three seconds, there is agreement within the uncertainty bounds between the current \gls{DNP} group parameters and most of the other results in the literature, with the Brady group parameters having a noticeable difference at longer times.

It is expected that once this data is added, the earlier times will fit more closely.

\subsubsection{Individual \glspl{DNP}}
The Monte Carlo sampling of each \glspl{DNP}' nuclear data allows for sensitivity analyses on how each change affects the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
For example, since group 1 is dominated by $^{87}$Br, then changes to the half-life, emission probability, or fission yield of $^{87}$Br would lead to noticeable changes in the group 1 \gls{DNP} half-life and yield.

The number of Monte Carlo simulations is increased to 5000 for this analysis, as the contribution of each \gls{DNP} needs sufficient data to ensure differences in \gls{DNP} group parameters are caused by its own sampled variation.
To find the nuclides with the most impactful sensitivities, a linear regression fits the slope of the relative change in \gls{DNP} group parameters to the relative change to the individual \gls{DNP} nuclear data, while also calculating the \gls{PCC}.
The \gls{PCC} describes the linear relationship between two variables on a scale from -1, representing a perfect negative correlation, +1 is a perfect positive correlation, and 0 is no correlation \cite{frey2018sage}. 
The changes in nuclear data include changes to emission probability, $P_n$; fission yields, which affect the concentration, $N$; and half-lives, $\tau$.

Sensitivities of interest in this analysis are for absolute values of the \gls{PCC} greater than 0.2.
This value is selected somewhat arbitrarily, but gives a useful indication that a nuclide has a non-negligible correlation with the \gls{DNP} group parameter of interest.

Another nuclide of interest in Table \ref{tab:pcc-analysis} is $^{86}$Ge, as it is also an important nuclide for calculating delayed neutron yield contributions as shown in Figure \ref{fig:delnu-dnp-contributions}.
It can be seen that $^{86}$Ge has an impact on many \gls{DNP} group parameters as there is variation in different parameters, especially its half-life.
Figures \ref{fig:ge86-0dscaled-sens-halflife} and \ref{fig:ge86-0dscaled-sens-yield} show the sensitivity to each \gls{DNP} group parameter to each variation in data for $^{86}$Ge.


\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{images/Half-life_Ge86.png}
    \caption{The difference in each \gls{DNP} group half-life from the $^{86}$Ge relative changes in emission probability, half-life, and concentration after a thermal saturation irradiation of $^{235}$U using the 0D scaled model.}
    \label{fig:ge86-0dscaled-sens-halflife}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:ge86-0dscaled-sens-halflife} has the largest sensitivities for the group half-lives when there are changes in the half-life of $^{86}$Ge.
It is interesting to note that, for increasingly longer-lived groups, the impact of the half-life of $^{86}$Ge on the group half-life becomes less and less.
This is intuitive, as a short-lived nuclide such as $^{86}$Ge should have much more impact on short-lived groups than on long-lived groups.
Another interesting pattern is that the group six half-life of $^{86}$Ge sensitivity appears to have a line below which there are no data points, indicating a lack of symmetry.
Sensitivities are typically symmetrical about the line that best fits the data for a linear response.
This asymmetry is likely due to the fact that there are many longer-lived \glspl{DNP}, whereas $^{86}$Ge is one of the few very short-lived \glspl{DNP} that dominates in this time region.
Because there aren't shorter-lived \glspl{DNP} in this time region, the half-life of group 6 does not decrease as much as it increases.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{images/Yield_Ge86.png}
    \caption{The difference in each \gls{DNP} group yield from the $^{86}$Ge relative changes in emission probability, half-life, and concentration after a thermal saturation irradiation of $^{235}$U using the 0D scaled model.}
    \label{fig:ge86-0dscaled-sens-yield}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:ge86-0dscaled-sens-yield} shows there are changes in the group yields based on changes in the half-life of $^{86}$Ge.
There is not a clear trend moving from shorter to longer-lived groups, though there are some negative correlations for groups three and four, which may indicate that a longer-lived $^{86}$Ge leads to a decreased yield in those groups while also increasing their half-lives, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:ge86-0dscaled-sens-halflife}.
It appears those groups capture fewer short-lived \glspl{DNP} as the half-life of $^{86}$Ge increases both by increasing their group half-lives and decreasing their yields.

Another interesting sensitivity correlation is the emission probability of $^{86}$Ge on the group six yield, which appears to have a narrow spread of values for small emission probabilities but then spreads out for larger emission probabilities.
The trend is positive because increasing the emission probability for the short-lived $^{86}$Ge corresponds to an increase in the short-lived group 6 yield.
One contributing factor to this spreading out of the yield is likely the lack of pulse irradiation data.
Because the short-lived groups are less accurate without this data, removing $^{86}$Ge leads to a more consistent yield in group six.
However, as $^{86}$Ge becomes more impactful, the possible value for the yield spreads out due to the lack of pulse irradiation data.
