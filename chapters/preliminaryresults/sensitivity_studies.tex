
This section examines \gls{DNP} group parameter sensitivity to various simulation parameters.
The evaluated simulation parameters are chemical removal, time nodes for the non-linear least squares problem, and nuclear data uncertainties.
For the chemical removal analysis, the \gls{MSBR} chemical reprocessing scheme is used.
The \gls{MSBR} reprocessing scheme has large chemical removal rates, providing a conservative impact of these chemical removal rates on the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
The time node evaluation includes the spacing between each node, the number of nodes, the total amount of time the sample should decay, and a combination of the total time and number of nodes.
Finally, the nuclear data uncertainties include uncertainty in the fission yields, half-lives, and emission probabilities.
To propagate these uncertainties to \gls{DNP} group parameters, the non-linear least squares solver runs 1000 times using random normal samples of each \gls{DNP}’s fission yield, half-life, and emission probability.

\subsection{Chemical Removal}

As shown in Appendix \ref{sec:appendixB}, the fraction of fuel salt in the ex-core region determines the scaling of the chemical removal rate.
This analysis assumes an ex-core fraction of 0.5.
This value is based on the core zone I, core zone II, and annulus salt volumes relative to primary system salt volume in the \gls{MSBR} \cite{robertson_conceptual_1971}.
Table \ref{tab:msbr_cycle_times} shows the \gls{MSBR} chemical reprocessing scheme used in this analysis, recreated here.

One consideration is that the 0D scaled model underestimates chemical removal rate effects.
This is because it only chemically removes from the cumulative fission yield of the element in the reprocessing scheme, and does not account for decay chains which may have chemical removal rates included.
For example, $^{137}$I has a cycle time of 60 days, corresponding to a chemical removal rate of $1.9\times 10^{-7}$ $[s^{-1}]$.
However, Sb and Te, which decay into I, have a chemical removal rate of 0.05 $[s^{-1}]$, many orders of magnitude larger than the I removal rate.
The 0D scaled model, with cumulative fission yields and no decay chain, does not capture this impact on the concentration of iodine.

\subsection{Time Nodes}
\label{sec:time-eval}
The time node sensitivity is important, as many nodes may be required to fully capture the physics, but each additional node increases computational cost.
Of interest are the node distribution, number of nodes, sample decay time, and combined decay time and number of nodes.

\subsubsection{Distribution}
Two time node distributions are compared: logarithmic spacing from 0.01 to 600 seconds and linear spacing from 0 to 600 seconds.
With 300 time nodes, linear spacing does not capture behavior in the first 2 seconds but provides higher resolution at longer time scales.
This longer time scale resolution is less useful, as many relevant \glspl{DNP} quickly drop off at longer time scales, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:important-dnp}.

Figure \ref{fig:time-distribution} compares \gls{DNP} group parameters for logarithmic and linear spacing.
Half-lives of shorter-lived groups are larger for linear spacing, consistent with expectations.
Linear spacing has no data between zero and two seconds, and it places greater weight on longer-lived groups due to the data distribution.
This causes shorter-lived groups to capture a larger yield, with yields shifted from groups two, three, and four to groups five and six.
The total \gls{DNP} yield differs by approximately 0.1\%, so changing time node spacing does not alter macroscopic \gls{DNP} group behavior.
Instead, it affects the distribution of delayed neutrons over time, with logarithmic spacing more accurate by capturing shorter-lived \glspl{DNP} effects.

\subsubsection{Total Decay Time}
\label{sec:total-decay-time}
Increasing total decay time produces two simultaneous effects: decreasing time node density, discussed in Section \ref{sec:time-tot-and-node}, and increasing the number of nodes evaluated at longer times.
Because the longest-lived \gls{DNP}\footnote{$^{211}$Tl, $^{210}$Tl, and $^{210}$Hg are longer-lived and have non-zero emission probabilities, but they live only tens of seconds longer and generally have negligible concentrations.}, $^{87}$Br, has a half-life of approximately one minute, it is reasonable to assume that the default final time of ten minutes offers sufficient data on all \gls{DNP} decays.

Figure \ref{fig:time-tot-dec-time} shows this assumption is not accurate.
The \gls{DNP} group parameters approach asymptotic values as the total decay time increases, with the decay time of 1200 seconds, or 20 minutes, being the smallest decay time after which longer decay times do not change the results.
The shorter total decay times inaccurately model \glspl{DNP}, as they do not fully capture long-lived \glspl{DNP} and have shorter group half-lives.
These shorter half-lives correlate with an overestimation of some longer-lived \gls{DNP} group yields.

\subsection{Nuclear Data Uncertainties}
A Monte Carlo approach evaluates \gls{DNP} group parameter sensitivity to nuclear data uncertainties.
For each nuclear data variable in the 0D scaled model, such as $^{87}$Br half-life, a random value is sampled from a normal distribution.
This process is repeated for every \gls{DNP} fission yield, half-life, and emission probability.

\subsubsection{Count Rate}
Figure \ref{fig:keepin-counts-0dscaled} shows count rate normalized to the Keepin et al. count rate.
The red lines show count rates from sampled simulations, while the blue dashed line shows the \gls{DNP} group parameters and uncertainty based on 1000 Monte Carlo non-linear least squares solves.
The black X's indicate the mean count rate at each point.
The other six-group \gls{DNP} parameters plotted include Keepin (1.0, as the plot is normalized to Keepin values), Brady, and Synetos \cite{keepin1957delayed, brady1989delayed, synetos1983delayed}.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/Keepin_counts.png}
    \caption{The delayed neutron count rate for thermal fission of $^{235}$U normalized to the Keepin et al. six-group \gls{DNP} group parameters.}
    \label{fig:keepin-counts-0dscaled}
\end{figure}

Beyond approximately three seconds, the current \gls{DNP} group parameters agree within uncertainty bounds with most literature results, with Brady parameters showing a noticeable difference at longer times.
Short time scales are inaccurate in the 0D scaled model due to the lack of pulse irradiation data, as discussed in the chapter introduction.
Adding this data is expected to improve the fit at earlier times.
To demonstrate this, the first two groups are used while the shorter-lived groups three through six are replaced with the Keepin et al. \gls{DNP} group parameters \cite{keepin1957delayed}.
Figure \ref{fig:keepin-counts-0dscaled-mod} shows this modified set of \gls{DNP} group parameters.

The modified \gls{DNP} group parameters are not a perfect comparison, as the non-linear least squares solve adjusts each group’s yield relative to the yields and half-lives of other groups.
This indicates excluding pulse irradiation data also affects longer-lived groups, though less than it affects shorter-lived groups.
Adjusting the shorter-lived groups improves agreement with the literature for the 0D scaled model, particularly at times less than one second.

\subsubsection{Individual \glspl{DNP}}
The Monte Carlo sampling of each \gls{DNP}'s nuclear data allows for sensitivity analyses on how each change affects the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
For example, because group 1 is dominated by $^{87}$Br, changes to its half-life, emission probability, or fission yield noticeably affect the group 1 \gls{DNP} half-life and yield.

The number of Monte Carlo simulations is increased to 5000 to provide sufficient data for each \gls{DNP}, ensuring differences in \gls{DNP} group parameters are statistically significant.
To find the nuclides with the most impactful sensitivities, a linear regression fits the slope of the relative change in \gls{DNP} group parameters to the relative change in the individual \gls{DNP} nuclear data, while also calculating the \gls{PCC}.
The \gls{PCC} quantifies the linear relationship between two variables, with -1 representing perfect negative correlation, +1 perfect positive correlation, and 0 no correlation \cite{frey2018sage}.
Changes in the nuclear data include emission probability $P_n$, fission yields (affecting concentration $N$), and half-lives $\tau$.

Sensitivities of interest have absolute \gls{PCC} values greater than 0.2.
This threshold is somewhat arbitrary but indicates a \gls{DNP} has a non-negligible correlation with a \gls{DNP} group parameter.

This is intuitive, as a short-lived nuclide such as $^{86}$Ge should have much more impact on short-lived groups than on long-lived groups.
Group six half-life sensitivity to $^{86}$Ge shows a lower bound with no data points, indicating asymmetry.
Sensitivities typically exhibit symmetry about the line that best fits the data for a linear response.
This asymmetry likely arises because many longer-lived \glspl{DNP} exist, whereas $^{86}$Ge is one of the few very short-lived \glspl{DNP} dominating this time region.
Because shorter-lived \glspl{DNP} are absent in this time region, group six half-life decreases less than it increases.

Figure \ref{fig:ge86-0dscaled-sens-yield} shows changes in the group yields due to changes in the $^{86}$Ge half-life.
No clear trend exists from shorter to longer-lived groups, though groups three and four show negative correlations, suggesting a longer-lived $^{86}$Ge decreases their yields while increasing their half-lives, with half-lives shown in Figure \ref{fig:ge86-0dscaled-sens-halflife}.
These groups capture fewer short-lived \glspl{DNP} as the $^{86}$Ge half-life increases, both by increasing their group half-lives and decreasing their yields.

Another interesting sensitivity is the $^{86}$Ge emission probability on the group six yield, showing a narrow spread for small emission probabilities that widens for larger emission probabilities.
Increasing the emission probability for the short-lived $^{86}$Ge corresponds to an increase in the short-lived group 6 yield.
This widening likely arises from the absence of pulse irradiation data.
Without this data, short-lived groups are less accurate, so removing $^{86}$Ge produces a more consistent group six yield.
As $^{86}$Ge becomes more impactful, possible group six yield values spread due to missing pulse irradiation data.
