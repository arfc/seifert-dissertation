
This section covers the sensitivity of the \gls{DNP} group parameters to various simulation parameters, including: chemical removal, time nodes at which to evaluate the non-linear least squares problem, and uncertainties in the data.
For the chemical removal analysis, the \gls{MSBR} chemical reprocessing scheme is used.
This is because it has large chemical removal rates, which will therefore provide a conservative estimate as to the impact these chemical removal rates may have on the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
The evaluation of the time nodes include the spacing between each node, the number of nodes, the total amount of time the sample should decay, and a combination of the total time and number of nodes.
Finally, the data uncertainties include uncertainty in the fission yields, half-lives, and emission probabilities.
To propagate these uncertainties to the \gls{DNP} group parameters, the non-linear least squares solver is run 1000 times with a random normal distribution sample taken from the fission yield, half-life, and emission probability of each \gls{DNP}.

\subsection{Chemical Removal}

As shown in Appendix \ref{sec:appendixB}, the fraction of fuel salt in the ex-core region determines the scaling of the chemical removal rate.
For this analysis, the ex-core fraction will be assumed to be 0.5.
This value is based on the approximate core zone I, core zone II, and annulus salt volume relative to the primary system salt volume in the \gls{MSBR} \cite{robertson_conceptual_1971}.
The chemical reprocessing scheme from the \gls{MSBR} used in this analysis is given in Table \ref{tab:msbr_cycle_times}.

One consideration is that the 0D scaled model will underestimate the effect of the chemical removal rate.
This is because it only chemically removes from the cumulative fission yield of the element in the reprocessing scheme, and does not account for decay chains which may have chemical removal rates included.
For example, $^{137}$I has a cycle time of 60 days, or a chemical removal rate of $1.9\times 10^{-7}$ $[s^{-1}]$.
However, Sb and Te, which both decay into I, also have a chemical removal rate of 0.05 $[s^{-1}]$, many orders of magnitudes larger than the I removal rate.
However, using the 0D scaled model, with cumulative fission yields and no decay chain, means that this effect is not captured.

\subsection{Time Nodes}
\label{sec:time-eval}
The time node sensitivity is important, as many time nodes may be required to capture the physics fully, but at an increasing cost for each additional time node.
Of interest are the distribution of nodes, the number of nodes, the amount of time the sample decays, and the combined decay time and number of nodes.

\subsubsection{Distribution}
For the distribution of time nodes, two different approaches are compared: a logarithmic spacing between 0.01 and 600 seconds, and a linear spacing between 0 and 600 seconds.
Because 300 time nodes are being used, this means that the linear spacing will not capture behavior which occurs in the first 2 seconds, but instead will have a higher resolution at the longer time scales.
This may not be as useful, as at longer time scales the number of \glspl{DNP} that are relevant drop away quickly, as discussed in Section  \ref{sec:important-dnp}.

Figure \ref{fig:time-distribution} shows how the \gls{DNP} group parameters compare between the logarithmic and linear spacing.
The half-lives of the shorter lived groups are measured as being larger for the linear spacing, which is consistent with expectations.
The linear spacing does not have any data between zero and two seconds, and there is greater weighting on the longer lived groups due to the data distribution.
This also leads to the shorter lived groups capturing a larger yield, with the yields being pulled from groups two, three, and four to be added to groups five and six.
The difference between the total \gls{DNP} yields is approximately 0.1\%, so changing the spacing of the time nodes doesn't change the macroscopic behavior of the \gls{DNP} groups.
Instead, it affects the distribution of delayed neutrons over time, where the logarithmic distribution is more accurate due to capturing the effects of shorter lived \glspl{DNP}.

\subsubsection{Total Decay Time}
\label{sec:total-decay-time}
As the total decay time increases, there are two effects occurring at the same time: a decrease in the time node density, discussed in Section \ref{sec:time-tot-and-node}, and an increase in the number of nodes evaluated at longer times.
Because the longest lived \gls{DNP}, $^{87}$Br, has a half-life of approximately one minute, it is reasonable to assume that the default final time of ten minutes offers sufficient data on all \gls{DNP} decays.

Figure \ref{fig:time-tot-dec-time} shows that this is true, where the differences in \gls{DNP} group parameters between a 600 second decay time and a 1000 second decay time is very small relative to the uncertainty bounds.
The short decay times do not accurately model the \glspl{DNP}, with underestimations of the half lives.
The half-life underestimations are then correlated with overestimation of some of the longer lived \gls{DNP} group yields.
The \gls{DNP} yield of groups two and four have an interesting trend, where they underestimate for short decay times and overestimate for intermediate decay times.
The cause of this is likely due to the simultaneous fitting of the surrounding groups, as the raw data itself remains unchanged.

\subsection{Nuclear Data Uncertainties}
To find the the sensitivity of the \gls{DNP} group parameters to the uncertainties in the nuclear data, a Monte Carlo approach is used.
From each piece of data used in the 0D scaled model, such as the half-life of $^{87}$Br, a random value would be sampled assuming a normal distribution.
This is repeated for every fission yield, half-life, and emission probability for every \gls{DNP}.

\subsubsection{Count Rate}
The count rate, normalized to the Keepin et al. count rate, is given in Figure \ref{fig:keepin-counts-0dscaled}.
The red circles indicate the count rates from various sampled simulations, while the blue dashed line indicates the \gls{DNP} group parameters used and the uncertainty based on the 1000 Monte Carlo sampled non-linear least squares solves.
The black X's indicate the mean count rate at each point.
The other six group \gls{DNP} parameters plotted include Keepin, which is 1.0 since the plot is normalized to the Keepin values, the Brady group parameters, and the Synetos group parameters \cite{keepin1957delayed, brady1989delayed, synetos1983delayed}.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/Keepin_counts.png}
    \caption{The delayed neutron count rate for thermal fission of $^{235}$U normalized to the Keepin et al. six-group \gls{DNP} group parameters.}

\end{figure}

This figure shows that beyond roughly three seconds, there is agreement within the uncertainty bounds between the current \gls{DNP} group parameters and most of the other results in the literature, with the Brady group parameters having a noticeable difference at longer times.
For the short time scales, as previously discussed in the introduction to this chapter, are inaccurate due to the lack of pulse irradiation data.
It is expected that once this data is added, the earlier times will fit more closely.
To demonstrate this, the first two groups are used while the shorter-lived groups three through six are replaced with the Keepin et al. \gls{DNP} group parameters \cite{keepin1957delayed}.
This modified set of \gls{DNP} group parameters can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:keepin-counts-0dscaled-mod}.

The modified \gls{DNP} group parameters are not a perfect comparison, as the non-linear least squares solve adjusts the yields of each group relative to the others and based on the half-lives of the others.
This means that not including pulse irradiation data will also affect the longer-lived groups, though by a smaller amount than the effects on the shorter-lived groups.
However, there is generally better agreement when adjusting the shorter lived groups, particularly during the times less than one second.

\subsubsection{Individual \glspl{DNP}}

The number of Monte Carlo simulations is increased to 5000 for this analysis, as the contribution of each \gls{DNP} needs sufficient data to ensure differences in \gls{DNP} group parameters are caused by its own sampled variation.
To find the nuclides with the most impactful sensitivities, a linear regression fits the slope of the relative change in \gls{DNP} group parameters to the relative change to the individual \gls{DNP} nuclear data.
In particular, sensitivities of interest are where the absolute value of the \gls{PCC}, which describes the correlation between data on a scale from -1 to 1, is greater than 0.2.
This value is selected somewhat arbitrarily, but gives a useful indication that a nuclide has a somewhat significant correlation with the \gls{DNP} group parameter of interest.

One of the most predictable sensitivities is $^{87}$Br on group one, as $^{87}$Br dominates this group.
Specifically, the sensitivities that affect the \gls{DNP} group parameters with a \gls{PCC} greater than 0.2 are the emission probability on the group yield, the fission yield on the group yield, and the half-life on the group half-life.
Surprisingly, there is also a correlation with group 3, where the emission probability of $^{87}$Br affects the \gls{DNP} group 3 half-life.
