
As part of this work, \gls{DNP} group parameter sensitivity studies were performed to evaluate chemical removal, time nodes for the non-linear least squares problem, and nuclear data uncertainties.
For the chemical removal analysis, the \gls{MSBR} chemical reprocessing scheme is used.
The \gls{MSBR} reprocessing scheme has large chemical removal rates, providing a conservative impact of these chemical removal rates on the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
The time node evaluation includes the spacing between each node, the number of nodes, the total amount of time the sample should decay, and a combination of the total time and number of nodes.
Finally, the nuclear data uncertainties include uncertainty in the fission yields, half-lives, and emission probabilities.
To propagate these uncertainties to \gls{DNP} group parameters, the non-linear least squares solver runs 1000 times using random normal samples of each \gls{DNP}’s fission yield, half-life, and emission probability.

One consideration is that the 0D scaled model, discussed in Section \ref{sec:data-model}, underestimates chemical removal rate effects.
This is because it only chemically removes from the cumulative fission yield of the element in the reprocessing scheme, and does not account for decay chains which may have chemical removal rates included.
For example, $^{137}$I has a chemical removal rate of $1.9\times 10^{-7}$ $[s^{-1}]$.
However, antimony and tellurium, which decay into iodine, have a chemical removal rate of 0.05 $[s^{-1}]$, many orders of magnitude larger than the iodine removal rate.
The 0D scaled model, with cumulative fission yields and no decay chain, does not capture this impact on the concentration of iodine.

\subsection{Time Discretization}
\label{sec:time-eval}
The discretization of the irradiation time is represented by some number of time nodes over the total decay time.
The time node sensitivity is important, as many nodes may be required to fully capture the physics, but each additional node increases computational cost.
Of interest are the node distribution, number of nodes, sample decay time, and combined decay time and number of nodes.

\subsubsection{Distribution}
Two time node distributions are compared: logarithmic spacing from 0.01 to 600 seconds and linear spacing from 0 to 600 seconds.
These distributions of nodes are compared because capturing short-lived \glspl{DNP} while maintaining sufficient resolution at longer time steps is of interest.
Comparing these node distributions can inform if logarithmic spacing provides sufficient accuracy for long-lived \gls{DNP} while capturing short-lived \gls{DNP}, as linear spacing will struggle to capture short-lived \gls{DNP}.
For example, linear spacing does not capture behavior in the first two seconds with 300 time nodes but provides higher resolution at longer time scales.
This longer time scale resolution is less useful, as many relevant \glspl{DNP} quickly drop off at longer time scales, as discussed in Section \ref{sec:important-dnp}.

Figure \ref{fig:time-distribution} compares \gls{DNP} group parameters for logarithmic and linear spacing.
Half-lives of shorter-lived groups are larger for linear spacing, consistent with expectations.
Linear spacing has no data between zero and two seconds, and it places greater weight on longer-lived groups due to the data distribution.
This causes shorter-lived groups to capture a larger yield, with yields shifted from groups two, three, and four to groups five and six.
This is because the underlying \gls{DNP} data is the same, causing the shorter-lived groups to capture the short-lived \glspl{DNP} as well as the longer-lived \glspl{DNP} due to their longer half-lives.
For example, even though group six has a longer half-life, it is still the shortest-lived group and captures the yield from the shortest-lived \glspl{DNP}.
Additionally, because it is longer-lived, it also captures more of the longer-lived \glspl{DNP}.

It appears there is not a significant difference between the results when varying the number of nodes.
The main difference appears in the yields of groups four and five, with increasing nodes increasing the yield of group four and decreasing the yield of group five.
There are also some smaller differences, such as the yield of group six decreasing and the yields of groups two and three increasing with an increasing number of nodes.

The \gls{DNP} group parameters with the most nodes are the most accurate, as many nodes provide the most accurate set of data for the \gls{DNP} group parameters to use for least squares fitting.
The logarithmic time node spacing means that the short-lived \gls{DNP} groups are captured, and the long-lived \gls{DNP} groups don't need as many data points to be captured.
Groups four and five are slightly longer-lived, and appear to require a sufficient number of nodes for their behavior to be captured fully.

\subsubsection{Total Time Post-Irradiation}
\label{sec:total-decay-time}
This analysis compares different lengths of time over which the sample decays, or total decay time, and measurements are collected for the same number of time nodes.
It is important to ensure the total length of time is accurate, as a longer decay time will introduce additional cost in the 0D flow model while a decay time that is too short will not fully capture the \glspl{DNP}.

Increasing the total decay time produces two simultaneous effects: decreasing time node density and increasing the number of nodes evaluated at longer times.
The decrease in time node density is because the same number of time nodes spread over a longer time frame will be less dense.
The increased number of time nodes at longer times can be understood by considering an extreme example of a distribution of 100 time nodes over 100 seconds compared to 100 time nodes over 100 years; even with logarithmic spacing, most of the nodes will be placed at a longer time in the 100 year case than the 100 second case.
This indicates that a longer total decay time can be expected to directly improve the longer-lived \gls{DNP} group parameters.

Because the longest-lived \gls{DNP}\footnote{The \glspl{DNP} $^{211}$Tl, $^{210}$Tl, and $^{210}$Hg are longer-lived and have non-zero emission probabilities, but they live only tens of seconds longer and generally have negligible concentrations.}, $^{87}$Br, has a half-life of approximately one minute, it is reasonable to assume that a final time of ten minutes offers sufficient data on all \gls{DNP} decays.
Figure \ref{fig:time-tot-dec-time} shows this assumption is not accurate.
The \gls{DNP} group parameters, $\tau_{k}$ and $\bar{\nu}_{d, k}$, converge asymptotically as the total decay time increases, with the decay time of 1200 seconds, or 20 minutes, being the smallest decay time after which longer decay times do not change the results.
The shorter total decay times inaccurately model \glspl{DNP}, as they do not fully capture long-lived \glspl{DNP} and have shorter group half-lives.
These shorter half-lives correlate with an overestimation of some longer-lived \gls{DNP} group yields.

\subsection{Nuclear Data Uncertainties}
This work uses a Monte Carlo approach to propagate the uncertainties in nuclear data and evaluate the sensitivity of the \gls{DNP} group parameters to these uncertainties.
For each nuclear data variable in the 0D scaled model, such as the $^{87}$Br half-life, a random value is sampled from a normal distribution.
This process is repeated for every \gls{DNP} fission yield, half-life, and emission probability.
For the analyses in this section, 5000 Monte Carlo non-linear least squares solves are used with 800 decay time nodes each.
The other parameters are the same as given in Table \ref{tab:default-0dsacled}.

The other six-group \gls{DNP} parameters plotted include Keepin (1.0, as the plot is normalized to Keepin values), Brady, and Synetos \cite{keepin1957delayed, brady1989delayed, synetos1983delayed}.

\subsubsection{Individual \glspl{DNP}}
The Monte Carlo sampling of each \gls{DNP}'s nuclear data performed in this work enables sensitivity analyses on how each change affects the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
For example, because group one is dominated by $^{87}$Br, changes to its half-life, emission probability, or fission yield noticeably affect the group one \gls{DNP} half-life and yield.

To find the most sensitive \glspl{DNP} that experience the most difference in \gls{DNP} group parameters $\tau_{k}$ and $\bar{\nu}_{d,k}$, a linear regression fits the slope of the relative change in \gls{DNP} group parameters to the relative change in the individual \gls{DNP} nuclear data, while also calculating the \gls{PCC}.
The \gls{PCC} quantifies the linear relationship between two variables: -1 indicates perfect negative correlation, +1 indicates perfect positive correlation, and 0 indicates no correlation \cite{frey2018sage}.
Changes in the nuclear data include emission probability $P_n$, fission yields (affecting concentration $N$), and half-lives $\tau$.

Correlations of interest for this analysis have absolute \gls{PCC} values greater than 0.2.
This threshold indicates a \gls{DNP} has at least a non-negligible correlation with a \gls{DNP} group parameter.

One of the most predictable sensitivities in Table \ref{tab:pcc-analysis} is $^{87}$Br’s effect on group one half-life and yield, as it dominates this group.
Specifically, $^{87}$Br, with a half-life of $55.64 \pm 0.15$ seconds, has correlations with \glspl{PCC} greater than 0.2 that impact the following group parameters: $^{87}$Br's emission probability and concentration affect the group one yield and $^{87}$Br's half-life affects the group one half-life.
Figure \ref{fig:br87-sens-0dscaled} shows how group one parameters vary with $^{87}$Br data.
The figure also shows an example of weakly correlated data using the $^{87}$Br emission probability on the group three half-life, though even this weak correlation has some non-negligible impact.

Although it may seem counterintuitive, changes to the $^{87}$Br emission probability affect the group three half-life, showing a non-negligible correlation.
The impact of $^{87}$Br on group one is expected, as well as some differences in group two, but even changes to the emission probability of $^{87}$Br has an effect on the half-life of group three.
This is because the \gls{DNP} group parameters cannot be considered in isolation; they form a collective set that best fits the delayed neutron count rate curve.
Changes to that curve propagate to all group parameters, with varying magnitudes across groups and parameters.

A metric to compare the overall sensitivity of the group parameters to nuclear data for each \gls{DNP} is the summed magnitude of the \glspl{PCC}, $\sum \left|PCC \right|$.
This value provides insight into how changing the nuclear data for a given \gls{DNP} affects all group parameters.
Figure \ref{fig:summedpccs} shows these values for each \gls{DNP}, where the theoretical maximum value would be a magnitude of one for each group half-life and yield, resulting in a net value of 12.
A value of 12 would indicate any change to the \gls{DNP}'s nuclear data results in a linear response in every \gls{DNP} group parameter.
The largest value is for $^{86}$Ge, with a value of approximately 8.1, indicating that uncertainties in $^{86}$Ge will have a large impact on the \gls{DNP} group parameters.

These \glspl{PCC} can be combined with the relative uncertainty associated with each \gls{DNP} to capture the nuclides which both impact the \gls{DNP} group parameters most significantly and have large uncertainties.
Specifically, this is calculated as the relative uncertainties times the magnitude of the \gls{PCC} for a \gls{DNP} $i$ and \gls{DNP} value $v$, or $U_{i,v}$:
\begin{equation}
    U_{i,v} = \frac{\sigma_{i,v}}{x_{i,v}} \sum_g\left|PCC_{i,v,g}\right|,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    U_{i,v} &= \text{the uncertainty-weighted sensitivity of \gls{DNP} value $v$ for \gls{DNP} $i$ [-]} \nonumber \\
    \sigma_{i,v} &= \text{the uncertainty in the \gls{DNP} value $v$ for \gls{DNP} $i$ [-]} \nonumber \\
    x_{i,v} &= \text{the \gls{DNP} value of half-life, emission probability, or concentration ($v$) of \gls{DNP} $i$ [-]} \nonumber \\
    PCC_{i, v, g} &= \text{the \gls{PCC} for \gls{DNP} $i$ and value $v$ with \gls{DNP} group parameter $g$ [-].} \nonumber \\
\end{align}
